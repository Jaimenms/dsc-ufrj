\begin{abstract}
% <= 250 palavras, autocontido, sem referências.
Severe slugging in pipeline--riser systems combines phase disappearance, moving liquid and gas fronts, changes of flow pattern and cycles of hundreds of seconds, which makes it a demanding test for transient two-fluid simulators. This work asks what a one-dimensional, four-equation two-fluid model with pattern-dependent closures needs to reproduce the cycle when the flow pattern is identified dynamically along the whole system, and uses the answer to map the stability of a well--pipeline--riser system with a topside choke. A characteristic analysis shows that the closures are ill-posed in large parts of their domain; a low-void rule and an objective virtual-mass term leave the model hyperbolic in all but \SI{0.25}{\percent} of the states visited by a severe-slugging simulation, against \SI{22}{\percent} without the virtual mass. Of five spatial discretisations, only a staggered finite-volume scheme with phase-wise donor cells carries the cycle; integrating the non-conservative form loses liquid in one step per blowdown, whereas a conservative residual closes the mass balances to the Newton tolerance, and evaluating the face closures with the donor void fraction removes an odd--even mode and an unstable oscillation of the cells at the bend. A variable-order implicit integrator with physical bounds and range-scaled tolerances, driven by a compiled right-hand side with exact Jacobians, runs one hour of slugging in \tbd{minutes}. Linearised with the mass matrix of the conservative formulation, the steady state loses stability through a slow complex pair whose period is that of the simulated cycles: the onset of slugging is a Hopf bifurcation, at a choke opening that grows with the reservoir pressure. \tbd{On a grid of choke openings and reservoir pressures, the stable region ... Jansen, Shoham and Taitel; starting from the steady state or from a blocked line gives different answers in X cells.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Severe slugging is the cyclic blockage and blowout of a riser by liquid that accumulates at a low point of a production line \citep{schmidt1980,boe1981,pots1987}. When the gas in the line cannot carry the liquid up the riser, the liquid seals the riser base, the line pressurises while the riser fills, and once the pressure in the line overcomes the hydrostatic head the gas penetrates the riser and expels the liquid in a short burst, after which the liquid falls back and the cycle restarts \citep{taitel1986,fabre1990,taitel1990a}. The cycle loads separators with liquid surges, causes pressure oscillations along the system and reduces production. Choking at the top of the riser can stabilise the flow \citep{schmidt1979,jansen1996}, at the price of a back-pressure, which makes the stability boundary in the plane of choke opening and inflow conditions the central question for operation.

That boundary is a question of the stability of a steady state, and a few terms of the theory of dynamical systems will be used throughout \citep{strogatz2024,kuznetsov2004}. A steady state is \emph{stable} if every small perturbation of it decays. Linearised about the steady state, a perturbation is a sum of modes that evolve as $e^{\lambda t}$, where the $\lambda$ are the eigenvalues of the linearised system: the real part of an eigenvalue is the growth rate of its mode, the imaginary part its angular frequency, and the steady state is stable if all real parts are negative. As a parameter of the system changes --- here the choke opening --- the steady state can lose its stability where an eigenvalue crosses the imaginary axis; such a parameter value is a \emph{bifurcation}. In a \emph{Hopf bifurcation} the crossing is made by a pair of complex-conjugate eigenvalues, $\lambda = \sigma \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega$ with $\sigma$ changing sign and $\omega \neq 0$: beyond it a perturbation grows as an oscillation of period $2\pi/\omega$, until the non-linear terms bound it in a periodic oscillation of finite amplitude, a \emph{limit cycle}, whose period near the bifurcation is $2\pi/\omega$. When the limit cycle grows from zero amplitude at the bifurcation (a supercritical bifurcation), the transition is gradual; when a cycle of finite amplitude already exists before it (subcritical), the stable steady state and the cycle coexist over a range of the parameter, and which one the system reaches depends on where it starts --- a \emph{hysteresis}. Further from the bifurcation the two eigenvalues of the pair may meet on the real axis and become real and positive. The oscillation is then a \emph{relaxation oscillation} \citep{vanderpol1926}: a slow accumulation followed by a fast discharge, whose period is set by the accumulation rather than by an eigenvalue. A severe-slugging cycle, with its slow pressurisation of the line and its blowout, has this shape. Section~\ref{sec:toy} illustrates each of these terms on a model problem.

Models of severe slugging range from lumped descriptions of the cycle \citep{taitel1990a,sarica1991} and low-order models for control \citep{storkaas2007,jahanshahi2011}, through drift-flux formulations and their nonlinear stability analysis \citep{balino2010,azevedo2023}, to one-dimensional two-fluid models of the kind used in commercial transient simulators \citep{bendiksen1991,pauchon1994,masella1998}, which have been compared with experiments in pipeline--riser systems and with choking \citep{malekzadeh2012,park2014,li2022}, to slug-capturing and slug-tracking studies \citep{issa2003,bonizzi2009,kjeldby2013,smith2016}. \citet{han2015} simulated terrain-induced severe slugging with a two-fluid model whose riser equations were made well-posed by an interfacial-pressure term, while the horizontal and descending sections were kept within the well-posed region of their closures. The two-fluid model is the natural framework, because it represents the slip between the phases, the fronts and the flow-pattern changes that a cycle involves, but it brings well-known difficulties: the model is not hyperbolic for every state and closure \citep{stuhmiller1977,ramshaw1978,lopezdebertodano2017}, phases appear and disappear, the closures change with the flow pattern, and the dynamics combine acoustic and void-wave time scales with a cycle of hundreds of seconds. Commercial codes resolve these difficulties in ways that are not published in full, and open studies usually prescribe the flow pattern of each section of the system.

This article reports what was needed to make a four-equation two-fluid model with pattern-dependent closures \citep{souza2010} simulate severe slugging with the flow pattern identified dynamically along the whole system, from the well inflow to the separator, and what can then be learnt from it about the stability of a well--pipeline--riser system with a topside choke. Section~\ref{sec:toy} first presents the method of analysis on a model problem with two linear equations and a single nonlinear boundary condition. The presentation then follows the chain of decisions in the order in which they matter: the model and its well-posedness (Sections~\ref{sec:model} and \ref{sec:wellposed}), the spatial discretisation (Section~\ref{sec:space}), the time integration and what happens when its ingredients are removed (Section~\ref{sec:time}), the compiled backend (Section~\ref{sec:backend}), and the application to a well--pipeline--riser system (Section~\ref{sec:application}). The contributions are:
(i) a dynamic flow-pattern identification, smooth and evaluated on the local state, that can be used inside an implicit transient solver;
(ii) a characteristic analysis by flow pattern, a regularisation built from physical terms, and a measure of well-posedness along the simulated trajectories rather than on a map;
(iii) a comparison of collocated and staggered discretisations and of primitive and conservative time integration on the same severe-slugging problem, with exact mass balances, and the identification of an odd--even mode created by inconsistent face void fractions;
(iv) an implicit integrator and a compiled right-hand side with exact Jacobians that make maps of hundreds of long transients affordable;
(v) the linear stability of the steady state of the discretised system, with the mass matrix that a conservative formulation requires, which predicts the onset and the period of the cycles;
(vi) profiles, fronts and flow patterns along the cycle, and a stability map in choke opening and reservoir pressure, with its hysteresis, compared with the linear boundary and with the criterion of \citet{jansen1996}.
Section~\ref{sec:lessons} collects the practical recommendations.

\section{A model problem}\label{sec:toy}

The analysis that this article applies to severe slugging --- a semi-discretisation in conservative variables, the linearisation of the steady state with its mass matrix, a continuation in the choke opening and transient simulations that test the linear predictions --- can be followed step by step on a much simpler problem with the same structure. A line $0 < x < 1$ carries a pressure $p$ and a flux $w$ governed by the telegraph equations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toyline}
C\pd{p}{t} + \pd{w}{x} = 0, \qquad L\pd{w}{t} + \pd{p}{x} = -R\,w,
\end{equation}
two linear partial differential equations with constant coefficients, hyperbolic with wave speeds $\pm 1/\sqrt{LC}$. The line is fed at $x = 0$ by a linear source, $w(0,t) = k\,(P_r - p(0,t))$, and closed at $x = 1$ by an element with a single state $y$, the flux through it,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toyend}
m\,\frac{\dd y}{\dd t} = p(1,t) - f(y) - a\,y, \qquad w(1,t) = y, \qquad f(y) = g_0 - \beta\,(y - y_0) + \tfrac{1}{3}(y - y_0)^3 .
\end{equation}
The line stands for the pipeline and its gas pocket, the source for the inflow from the reservoir, the end element for the riser and the resistance $a$ for the choke. The characteristic $f$ of the end element falls with the flux around $y_0$, as the head of a riser that lightens when more gas flows through it, and the cubic term bounds it; it is the only nonlinearity of the problem. A transmission line terminated by such a negative-resistance element is the tunnel-diode line of \citet{nagumo1961}, a close relative of the equations of \citet{fitzhugh1961} and \citet{nagumo1962}, and its oscillations are those of \citet{vanderpol1926}.

The line is discretised as the two-fluid model will be (Section~\ref{sec:space}): $p$ at the centres of 50 cells, $w$ at the faces, the inlet face given by the source and the outlet face by $y$. The semi-discrete right-hand side returns the time derivatives of the charges $C p$, of the momenta $L w$ and of $m y$, as the conservative formulation of the two-fluid model returns those of the phase masses, so that the linearisation about a steady state is the generalised eigenvalue problem $\bJ\bv = \lambda\bM\bv$ with $\bM = \mathrm{diag}(C, L, m)$. A lumped approximation shows what to expect. If the inertance and the friction of the line are neglected, the pressure is uniform along it, $p(x,t) \approx P(t)$, and integrating the first of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:toyline} over the line leaves two ordinary differential equations, for $P$ and for the flux $y$ through the end element:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toylumpedode}
C\,\frac{\dd P}{\dd t} = k\,(P_r - P) - y, \qquad m\,\frac{\dd y}{\dd t} = P - f(y) - a\,y .
\end{equation}
At the steady state $(P^*, y^*)$ the inflow equals the outflow, $k\,(P_r - P^*) = y^*$, and the pressure balances the end element, $P^* = f(y^*) + a\,y^*$. Small deviations $\delta P$ and $\delta y$ from it obey
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toylumped}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix}\delta P \\ \delta y\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{B}\begin{pmatrix}\delta P \\ \delta y\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix} -k/C & -1/C \\ 1/m & -r/m \end{pmatrix}, \qquad r = a + f'(y^*),
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the net differential resistance of the end element: the resistance $a$ of the `choke' plus the slope of the `riser' characteristic, which is negative near $y_0$. The factors $1/C$ and $1/m$ are the inverse of the mass matrix of this two-state system, so that $\mathbf{B}$ is its $\bM^{-1}\bJ$. The steady state is stable while the trace of $\mathbf{B}$, $-k/C - r/m$, is negative and its determinant, $(1 + k\,r)/(Cm)$, is positive. As $a$ decreases, $r$ decreases, and the trace vanishes at $r = -mk/C$: a pair of complex eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis, a Hopf bifurcation in which the end element oscillates against the capacitance of the line with the frequency $\sqrt{\det\mathbf{B}} \approx 1/\sqrt{Cm}$. The two eigenvalues become real when the discriminant of $\mathbf{B}$, $(k/C - r/m)^2 - 4/(Cm)$, vanishes, at $r = mk/C - 2\sqrt{m/C}$; between the two values of $r$ the steady state is an unstable focus, below the second an unstable node.

Fig.~\ref{fig:toy} follows the problem with $C = 10$, $L = R = 0.05$, $k = 0.5$, $m = 0.1$, $\beta = g_0 = y_0 = 1$ and $P_r$ such that $y^* = 1$ at $a = 1$. A large resistance stabilises the line. As $a$ decreases, the slow pair crosses the imaginary axis at $a_H = 0.976$ with a period of 6.75 (the lumped approximation gives 0.995 and 6.29), and its two eigenvalues meet on the real axis at $a_R = 0.756$ (0.792), below which two real global modes are unstable (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}b). The transients, integrated with the implicit formula of Section~\ref{sec:time} from the steady state perturbed by \SI{0.1}{\percent}, follow the spectrum. Above $a_H$ the perturbation decays. Just below, it grows into a nearly sinusoidal cycle with the period of the pair (6.8 at $a = 0.95$, against 6.83 predicted), which widens as $a$ decreases (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}a, d). Below $a_R$ the cycle becomes a relaxation cycle: the line charges slowly while the flux through the end element stays on the left branch of $f$, the flux jumps to the right branch and the line discharges, with a flux wave that runs from the end into the line (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}e), and the flux jumps back, overshooting to negative values. Its period, 12.3 at $a = 0.5$, is set by the charging of the line, not by the pair, which no longer exists. In the order in which the choke opens, these are the stable flow, the pressure cycle and the severe slugging of Section~\ref{sec:application}, including the backflow through the choke at the end of a blowout.

The mass matrix matters. The eigenvalues of $\bJ$ alone, the Jacobian of the time derivatives of the charges and momenta, are those of a system in which $C$, $L$ and $m$ were all set to one: their largest real part stays at $-0.025$ for every $a$, and the line would be declared stable at every opening (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}b).

What the model problem does not have is the rest of this article: the characteristics of the two-fluid model can become complex, phases vanish, the masses are non-linear functions of the state (with a constant capacitance the primitive and conservative formulations coincide; with one that depends on the pressure, as for a gas, they do not), and the closures change with the flow pattern. The following sections take these up in the order of the introduction.

\section{Mathematical model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two-fluid equations}\label{sec:equations}

The gas ($k = G$) and liquid ($k = L$) phases flow in a pipe of constant cross-section $A$ and inclination $\theta(x)$. With the area fractions $\aG + \aL = 1$, the phase mass flow rates $q_k = \rho_k \alpha_k A v_k$ and a common pressure $P$, the mass and momentum balances are
\begin{gather}
\pd{}{t}\left(\rho_k \alpha_k A\right) + \pd{q_k}{x} = 0, \label{eq:mass}\\
\pd{q_k}{t} + \pd{}{x}\left(q_k v_k\right) = -\alpha_k A \pd{P}{x} - A\pd{}{x}\left(\alpha_k \Delta P_k\right) - \Gamma_k - \rho_k \alpha_k A g \sin\theta + F^{vm}_k, \label{eq:momentum}
\end{gather}
where $\Delta P_k$ is the difference between the average pressure of phase $k$ and $P$ (the hydrostatic level gradient in stratified flow, the interfacial pressure of dispersed flow), $\Gamma_k$ the wall and interfacial shear forces per unit length and $F^{vm}_k$ the virtual-mass force. Each phase follows a polytropic law, $\rho_k = \rho_{k0}(P/P_0)^{1/\gamma_k}$, with $\gamma_G$ close to one for the gas and a large $\gamma_L$ for the liquid. For the analysis the system is written in the primitive variables $\by = (\aG, P, q_L, q_G)$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quasilinear}
\pd{\by}{t} + \bA(\by)\,\pd{\by}{x} + \mathbf{b}(\by) = \mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
whose matrix $\bA$ contains the convective terms, the pressure coupling through the phase compressibilities and the derivatives of $\Delta P_k$ with respect to $\aG$ and $P$. The virtual-mass force acts in dispersed bubbly flow and is written in the objective form of \citet{drew1979},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vm}
F^{vm}_G = -F^{vm}_L = -C_{vm}\, w_B\, \aG \rho_L A \left[\frac{D_G v_G}{Dt} - \frac{D_L v_L}{Dt} + (v_G - v_L)\left((\lambda - 2)\pd{v_G}{x} + (1 - \lambda)\pd{v_L}{x}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
with $C_{vm} = 0.5$, $\lambda = 1$ and $w_B$ the weight of the bubbly pattern (Section~\ref{sec:patterns}).

\subsection{Closure relations}\label{sec:closures}

The closures follow the flow pattern. In \emph{stratified} flow the wetted perimeters and the hydrostatic pressure corrections follow from the liquid level \citep{taitel1976}, the wall friction from the Fanning factor of \citet{churchill1977} on each phase's hydraulic diameter, and the interfacial friction from \citet{andritsos1987}. In \emph{annular} flow the film friction follows \citet{kowalski1987} and the interfacial friction the same correlation on the film thickness. In \emph{bubbly} flow the bubble diameter is limited by the Hinze--Calderbank breakup diameter \citep{hinze1955}, the terminal velocity follows \citet{mendelson1967} and \citet{fan1990}, the interfacial drag balances buoyancy at the terminal velocity, and the interfacial pressure is $\Delta P_L = 0.25\rho_L (v_G - v_L)^2$ \citep{stuhmiller1977}. In \emph{intermittent} flow a slug unit made of an aerated liquid slug and a stratified film region \citep{taitel1990b} weighs the bubbly and stratified closures by the lengths of the two regions, with the slug-body holdup of \citet{barnea1985}, the translational velocity of \citet{bendiksen1984}, the slug length of \citet{nicholson1978} and the slug frequency of \citet{zabaras2000}. Appendix~\ref{app:closures} gives the expressions. Where a phase vanishes, its velocity is undefined; Section~\ref{sec:space} treats this numerically.

\subsection{Dynamic flow-pattern identification}\label{sec:patterns}

The flow pattern is identified at every cell and every time from stability criteria: the Kelvin--Helmholtz criterion for the stability of stratified flow and the liquid-film trajectory criterion for downward flow \citep{taitel1976,barnea1982}, wind-generated waves for the wavy subpattern, the film-bridging and Froude criteria for annular flow \citep{taitel1980,barnea1987}, and the dispersion and coalescence criteria that separate bubbly from intermittent flow \citep{taitel1980,barnea1987}. The criteria, listed in Appendix~\ref{app:patterns}, are combined into weights $w_S$, $w_A$, $w_B$, $w_I$ of the stratified, annular, bubbly and intermittent closures,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weights}
w_A = (1 - w_S)\,p_A, \qquad w_B = (1 - w_S)(1 - w_A)\,p_B, \qquad w_I = (1 - w_S)(1 - w_A)(1 - p_B),
\end{equation}
so that $w_S + w_A + w_B + w_I = 1$ for any value of the indicators. The closure terms are the weighted sums of the four closures.

Steady-state flow-pattern maps compare superficial velocities with equilibrium solutions of the stratified and annular momentum balances; used as they are inside a transient solver they fail in three ways, which the present identification avoids. First, a sharp switch of the closures makes the residual non-smooth, and a Newton iteration then alternates between patterns from one iteration to the next; the indicators are therefore logistic functions whose width is a fraction of the critical value, $1/(1 + e^{-k (X - X_c)/|X_c|})$ with $k = 20$, so that criteria of very different magnitudes switch over the same relative interval. Second, the equilibrium level computed from superficial velocities does not describe a cell whose content is far from equilibrium: a gas pocket at rest in front of a liquid column has small superficial velocities and would be classified as bubbly flow. The criteria are therefore evaluated on the local state: the actual level and void fraction replace the equilibrium ones in the stratified criteria, the bubbly criterion uses the larger of the drift-flux estimate and the cell void fraction, the film-bridging criterion of annular flow uses the larger of the equilibrium film fraction and the liquid fraction of the cell (a cell that holds more liquid than a bridging film cannot be annular, however small the superficial velocities), stratified flow is excluded in vertical sections and annular flow is assigned above $\aG = 0.9$. Third, when the gas fraction is very small no pattern can be identified, since the criteria degenerate; below $\aG = 0.01$ the closures blend smoothly into the bubbly ones (low-void rule; Section~\ref{sec:wellposed}).

\subsection{Well inflow, choke and separator}\label{sec:bc}

The pipe receives the reservoir fluids through a linear inflow performance relation, $q_k = J_k\max(0, P_{res} - P_{in})$, with $J_G/J_L$ equal to the gas--liquid mass ratio of the reservoir fluid, and discharges through a choke into a separator at fixed pressure. The choke follows a homogeneous law: the mixture crosses the valve with the no-slip density weighted by volume, $\Delta P = \dot m\, Q/(c_v\, a)^2$, with $\dot m$ and $Q$ the mass and volumetric flow rates, $a$ the opening and $c_v$ the valve coefficient. A density weighted by mass flow, $\rho_M = (q_L\rho_L + q_G\rho_G)/(q_L + q_G)$, which is sometimes used, makes the choke almost transparent to gas, because the gas carries little mass, and becomes singular when the gas flows back through the choke at the end of a blowout and $q_L + q_G$ crosses zero; with it the choke does not stabilise the riser (Section~\ref{sec:map}).

\section{Well-posedness}\label{sec:wellposed}

\subsection{Characteristic analysis by flow pattern}\label{sec:characteristics}

The system~\eqref{eq:quasilinear} is hyperbolic when the eigenvalues of $\bA$ are real; complex eigenvalues make the initial-value problem ill-posed, with growth rates proportional to the wavenumber, so that the numerical solution does not converge as the mesh is refined \citep{lopezdebertodano2017}. Fig.~\ref{fig:hyperbolicity} maps the largest imaginary part of the characteristic velocities over void fraction and slip for each closure at the conditions of the riser base of the application (\SI{6}{\bar}, $v_L = \SI{0.5}{\metre\per\second}$), and Table~\ref{tab:hyperbolicity} gives the ill-posed fractions. Four regions are ill-posed. The stratified closure loses hyperbolicity when the gas layer becomes very thin, $\aG \lesssim 0.005$, for any slip: the hydrostatic level gradient, which makes stratified flow hyperbolic up to the inviscid Kelvin--Helmholtz limit \citep{barnea1994}, vanishes when the level reaches the top of the pipe. This is precisely the state in which every liquid-filled cell is evaluated, since the void fraction of a cell without gas is cut at a small positive value. The bubbly closure is ill-posed at low void fraction with slip, the classical result of \citet{stuhmiller1977}. The annular closure, with both phases at the same pressure, is ill-posed for almost any slip, and the intermittent closure at low void fraction and at large slip.

\subsection{Regularisation}\label{sec:regularisation}

Three terms remove the ill-posed regions without changing the equations elsewhere. The low-void rule blends the closures of any pattern into the bubbly ones below $\aG = 0.01$ with a $C^1$ ramp, which removes the thin-layer limit of the stratified closure; physically, a stratified gas layer of one per cent of the cross-section is not a flow pattern. The objective virtual-mass term \eqref{eq:vm} makes bubbly flow hyperbolic for every slip \citep{drew1979}; with $\lambda = 2$, which keeps only the difference of material accelerations, the bubbly map becomes \emph{more} ill-posed (\SI{99}{\percent} of the map against \SI{46}{\percent} without virtual mass), so the objective terms are essential. An interfacial-pressure term of the form proposed by \citet{bestion1990}, $\Delta P_i = \delta\, \aG \aL \rho_G \rho_L (v_G - v_L)^2/(\aG\rho_L + \aL\rho_G)$ with $\delta \geq 1$, makes the annular and intermittent closures hyperbolic (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyperbolicity}d, h), but has no physical basis in those patterns and does not act on the thin stratified layer, where it is proportional to $\aG$. Surface tension adds a third-order dispersive term that limits the growth of capillary waves at millimetre scale; it does not change the characteristics and is irrelevant at cell sizes of metres.

\subsection{Well-posedness along the simulated trajectories}\label{sec:trajectories}

A map shows where a closure is ill-posed, not whether a simulation goes there. The eigenvalues of $\bA$ were therefore evaluated in every cell and at every output time of the simulations of Section~\ref{sec:application}. For the severe-slugging run with the choke open (\SI{6000}{\second}, one state every \SI{5}{\second}), \SI{0.25}{\percent} of the cell--time states are ill-posed with the low-void rule and the virtual mass. Nearly all of them are intermittent or annular cells (\SI{2.2}{\percent} and \SI{1.7}{\percent} of the states with those patterns), \SI{89}{\percent} of them in the riser during the blowouts, with imaginary parts of the characteristic velocities of \SI{0.24}{\metre\per\second} (median). \section{Spatial discretisation}\label{sec:space}

\subsection{Schemes}\label{sec:schemes}

Five discretisations were applied to the same problem. Four are collocated, with the four primitive variables at the same nodes: the Galerkin and Petrov--Galerkin finite-element methods, and central and backward finite differences, the latter applied to all variables, the pressure included. The fifth is a staggered finite-volume scheme in the spirit of \citet{harlow1965} and \citet{patankar1980}: void fraction and pressure at the cell centres, phase mass flow rates at the faces. The mass fluxes and the momentum flux of each phase use the donor cell defined by the sign of that phase's flow rate, the pressure gradient is centred between neighbouring cells, and the pressure corrections $\Delta P_k$ are differenced between cell centres.

\subsection{What a severe-slugging cycle demands}\label{sec:demands}

A cycle requires that a front between a liquid column and a gas pocket travel through the bend and up the riser, that a phase vanish from a cell and reappear, and that the inventory of both phases be conserved over many cycles. Table~\ref{tab:discretisation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:discretisation} compare the five schemes on the application case with the choke open, the staggered one with both time formulations of Section~\ref{sec:conservation}. The collocated schemes have no dissipation on the void waves and develop node-to-node oscillations at the fronts; the backward difference applied to the pressure is not an upwind scheme for the acoustic waves, which travel in both directions, and it is unstable. None of the collocated schemes reaches the first blowout, about \SI{70}{\second} after the start. The backward differences and the Petrov--Galerkin method fail in the first steps (\SI{0.04}{\second} and \SI{0.4}{\second}), at the discontinuity of the initial state between the gas-filled line and the liquid-filled bend; the central differences carry the front for \SI{19}{\second} while node-to-node oscillations grow in the bend and the riser, where the void fraction alternates between 0.001 and 0.8 from one node to the next when the integrator fails. The Galerkin method does not fail but crawls: about 20\,000 steps per simulated second, more than 200 times as many as the staggered scheme, bring it to \SI{13.6}{\second} within the time budget of the comparison, with node-to-node oscillations of the void fraction along the line and in the bend; the staggered scheme completes \SI{3000}{\second} with both time formulations.

\subsection{Vanishing phases}\label{sec:vanishing}

In a liquid-filled cell the gas momentum equation has no mass and the gas flow rate is undetermined. Three choices proved necessary. The mass flux of a phase through a face is multiplied by a $C^1$ limiter of the donor cell's fraction of that phase, so that a cell without a phase cannot give it away; this keeps the inventories non-negative without limiting the void fraction itself. A limiter on the void fraction, the obvious alternative, destroys mass whenever it acts and produces cycles that are artefacts of the lost liquid. The flow rate of the vanishing phase is relaxed, with a time constant of \SI{0.05}{\second}, to the value it would have without slip, computed with the donor cell's fraction floored at the small value at which the closures see the phase. Relaxing it to a zero flow rate instead leaves a virtual gas layer at rest above the moving liquid; a stratified closure evaluated in that cell sees a gas layer sheared by the liquid and exerts on a liquid-filled pipe an interfacial friction tens of times the wall friction, which slows a liquid column down. With the low-void rule of Section~\ref{sec:regularisation} the cell is evaluated with the bubbly closures and the two targets give the same cycle (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}); the no-slip target remains the one that does not depend on that rule. Finally, an artificial viscosity $\nu = C c h$ in the momentum equations, with $c$ the phase sound speed, $h$ the cell size and $C = 0.05$, damps the acoustic ringing of the short liquid columns that form at the riser base after a blowout, which otherwise forces time steps of milliseconds; it vanishes as $h \to 0$ and does not affect waves much longer than a cell.

\subsection{Face void fraction and an odd--even mode}\label{sec:oddeven}

In the momentum equation of a face, the area fraction that multiplies the pressure gradient and the gravity term is that of the donor cell, while the closures (wall and interfacial friction) are naturally evaluated with the state at the face. If that state takes the arithmetic mean of the void fractions of the two neighbouring cells, the steady momentum balance of each face relates the donor fraction to the mean one; with the donor fraction $\alpha_i$ given, the balance fixes $(\alpha_i + \alpha_{i+1})/2$, so that $\alpha_{i+1} \approx 2\alpha^\ast - \alpha_i$ and the discrete steady state admits an odd--even mode. In the riser the void fraction then alternates from cell to cell with an amplitude that grows upwards (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}a). Evaluating the face closures with the donor void fraction, defined by the sign of the mixture volumetric flux, makes every term of the face balance use the same fraction: the steady profile becomes smooth, and an unstable oscillation localised in the cells of the bend disappears from the linearised steady state (Section~\ref{sec:linear}). \tbd{The two choices give cycles that differ on the coarse mesh and converge with mesh refinement (Section~\ref{sec:mesh}).}

\subsection{Conservation}\label{sec:conservation}

The staggered scheme conserves mass cell by cell in space, but conservation in time depends on the variables that the time integrator advances. Integrating the primitive form, $\partial\aG/\partial t$ and $\partial P/\partial t$, with an implicit multistep formula does not conserve the phase masses, which are non-linear functions of $\aG$ and $P$. The error is not uniform in time: it accumulates in steps, one per blowout, when a phase vanishes from many cells within a few seconds (Fig.~\ref{fig:discretisation}c). After \SI{3000}{\second} the primitive formulation has lost \SI{1.3}{\kilogram} of liquid and created \SI{15}{\gram} of gas, \SI{0.08}{\percent} of the liquid and \SI{0.45}{\percent} of the gas fed; spikes of the inlet pressure of up to \SI{0.5}{\bar} appear at some of the steps, and the cycles drift apart from those of the conservative formulation after the second blowout (Fig.~\ref{fig:discretisation}b). The conservative formulation applies the multistep formula to the masses per unit volume, $\bT(\bx) = (\rho_G\aG, \rho_L\aL)$, while Newton's method still iterates on the primitive variables,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservative}
\bT(\bx_{n+1}) - \sum_{j} a_j\, \bT(\bx_{n-j}) - \gamma h\, \bF(\bx_{n+1}) = \mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
with the Jacobian $\partial\bT/\partial\bx$ evaluated analytically. The mass balances then close to the tolerance of the Newton iterations: the errors stay below \SI{5e-4}{\kilogram}, $3\times10^{-7}$ of the liquid and $4\times10^{-6}$ of the gas fed. The map $\bT$ is singular at $\aG = -\gamma_G/\gamma_L$, so the void fraction is allowed a small undershoot, $\aG \geq -0.005$, well inside that limit. Verifying the balance itself requires care: integrating the boundary fluxes over the output times with the trapezoidal rule leaves an error of the order of a kilogram in each blowout, which is quadrature error and not loss of mass; the boundary fluxes must be integrated with the same multistep recurrence as the states.

\section{Time integration}\label{sec:time}

\subsection{Integrator}\label{sec:bdf}

The semi-discrete system is integrated by a backward differentiation formula of order one or two with variable step \citep{hairer1996}, a modified Newton method with the Jacobian reused while the convergence is fast, and an error estimate from the difference between the corrector and the predictor. Four features are not available in general-purpose stiff integrators and were needed here. The states are projected onto their physical bounds after each step, and the order falls back to one after a projection, since the history used by the second-order formula is inconsistent with a projected state. The absolute tolerance of each state is a fraction ($10^{-2}$) of the range that the state is expected to cover in the case (void fraction one, pressure up to the hydrostatic head of the riser, flow rates up to twenty times the liquid feed and fifty times the gas feed in a blowout), instead of a single value for states whose scales differ by four orders of magnitude; with a single tolerance the controller resolves the acoustic ringing of the liquid columns at a few per cent of the feed and the cost multiplies. The Newton iteration has a budget of twenty iterations, because its convergence is limited by the non-smoothness of the closures rather than by the quality of the Jacobian. The boundary fluxes are integrated with the same recurrence as the states, which gives an exact balance (Section~\ref{sec:conservation}).

\subsection{Steady states, continuation and adaptive end time}\label{sec:steady}

Steady states are computed by Newton's method on $\bF(\bx) = \mathbf{0}$. From a generic initial estimate, the iteration does not converge for this problem; from a state reached by a transient that settles, or from the steady state of a neighbouring point, it converges in a few iterations, and continuation in the choke opening and the reservoir pressure gives the steady state of every point of a map, stable or not. Pseudo-transient continuation is not used, because it converges only to stable equilibria \citep{kelley1998}. Transients that start from a steady state are perturbed by \SI{0.1}{\percent} in pressure, and their time step is bounded by \SI{1}{\second}. Without that bound the error control lets the step grow to hundreds of seconds while the solution is still close to the equilibrium, and a backward differentiation formula with $|\lambda h| \gg 1$ damps an unstable mode as well as a stable one: the trajectory stays on an unstable equilibrium and the run is classified as stable. In a first map of this kind, computed with the flow pattern prescribed by inclination, seven of the eight points in which the start from the steady state disagreed with the start from a blocked line had been integrated in 22 to 97 steps over \SI{2000}{\second}; with the step bounded, five of them cycle from both starts. The simulated time is adaptive: the integration proceeds in windows of \SI{2000}{\second} and stops when the inlet pressure stays within \SI{0.1}{\bar} or when two consecutive windows have the same amplitude and period within \SI{10}{\percent}, up to \SI{8000}{\second}. Even with the step bounded, a perturbation of \SI{0.1}{\percent} of the pressure that grows at \SI{e-3}{\per\second} needs more than \SI{2000}{\second}, the length of a window, to exceed a range of \SI{0.1}{\bar}, so that a transient from the steady state, stopped by such a rule, misses the slowest instabilities near the onset; the stability of a steady state is better decided by its eigenvalues (Section~\ref{sec:linear}).

\subsection{What breaks without each ingredient}\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} removes one ingredient at a time from the configuration of the application, with the choke wide open, and reports whether \SI{3000}{\second} of severe slugging are completed, at what cost, with which mass error and with which cycle; a run is stopped after \SI{5400}{\second} of wall-clock time, 1.8 times that of the reference. Four groups appear. Without the donor-cell limiter of the mass fluxes, without the acoustic viscosity, without the low-void rule, or with smooth switches evaluated on the equilibrium criteria, the integration crawls: it needs 2.2 to 7 times as many steps per simulated second as the reference and covers only 627 to \SI{2235}{\second} within the budget; with sharp switches on the equilibrium criteria it does not pass \SI{6}{\second}, at forty thousand steps per simulated second. Without the virtual mass the integrator fails after two blowouts (\SI{777}{\second}); with the choke density weighted by mass it fails after \SI{41}{\second}, as the liquid outflow of the first blowout stops and the total flow through the choke, which that density divides by, approaches zero; and the general-purpose BDF of SciPy, which integrates the primitive form with a single absolute tolerance and without projection on the bounds, fails after one second with a singular iteration matrix. Three changes complete the run but alter the result: the primitive time integration, which loses mass (Section~\ref{sec:conservation}); a single absolute tolerance, which costs \SI{31}{\percent} more steps; and a bubble drag without $g$ and $\sigma/\rho_L$, four times cheaper because the cycle degenerates into an oscillation of \SI{0.36}{\bar} instead of \SI{1.2}{\bar}. \section{Compiled backend}\label{sec:backend}

The fluid properties, closures, flow-pattern identification, schemes and network equations are written once against an injected array backend: the same code runs with NumPy and with JAX \citep{bradbury2018}, where the right-hand side is compiled just in time and its Jacobian is obtained exactly by forward-mode automatic differentiation \citep{griewank2008}. The two backends agree to a relative $10^{-14}$. Three rules make the physics differentiable and compilable: no in-place assignment, loops with a data-independent structure, and branches on values written as selections, with guards before functions with restricted domains so that tangents remain finite (a product $0 \times \infty$ in a tangent gives a NaN that only appears in the Jacobian). Table~\ref{tab:performance} compares the backends. The compiled right-hand side is \tbd{nine} times faster than the NumPy one, and the exact Jacobian \tbd{twenty} times faster than the coloured finite differences \citep{curtis1974} that need 25 evaluations of the right-hand side for the 112 states of the base mesh; \SI{1000}{\second} of severe slugging run in \tbd{X} instead of \tbd{X}. The exact Jacobian does not reduce the number of Newton iterations, which is set by the non-smoothness of the closures, so the gain is in the cost per step. On a CPU the compiled code is limited by latency, not by throughput: the systems have one to a few hundred unknowns, and parallelism pays between simulations, which is how the maps of Section~\ref{sec:map} were run.

\subsection{Linear stability of the steady state}\label{sec:linear}

In the conservative formulation the semi-discrete system reads $\bM(\bx)\,\dot\bx = \bF(\bx)$ with $\bM = \partial\bT/\partial\bx$, and its linearisation about a steady state is the generalised eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:geneig}
\bJ\,\bv = \lambda\,\bM\,\bv, \qquad \bJ = \pd{\bF}{\bx}.
\end{equation}
The eigenvalues of $\bJ$ alone have no meaning here: the rows of the mass balances are scaled by $\rho_L$ and $\rho_G$, and on a bubbly riser they differ by up to a factor of fifteen from those of the same scheme written in primitive variables, with which the eigenvalues of \eqref{eq:geneig} coincide to $10^{-9}$. For the present case the spectrum of $\bJ$ alone shows real unstable modes of 30 to \SI{40}{\per\second} at the low point at every opening, which no transient exhibits, and no slow mode that changes stability; with $\bM$ the picture is the opposite.

Fig.~\ref{fig:linear} shows the slow part of the spectrum at \SI{15}{\bar}, computed with the exact Jacobian along a continuation in the choke opening. At small openings every eigenvalue is stable. The slow eigenvalues form an arc of damped modes around \SI{-0.02}{\per\second}, the void waves of the line and the riser, from which one complex pair separates as the choke opens (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}b, c). Its eigenvector, with the states scaled by their ranges, is \SI{89}{\percent} void fraction, \SI{94}{\percent} of its norm in the bend and the riser, coupled to the pressure of the line: the liquid of the bend and riser exchanging volume with the compressible gas pocket, the mechanism of the cycle. Its growth rate increases monotonically with the opening and changes sign at $a_H = 0.24$, with a period of \SI{573}{\second} (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}d): the onset of the cycles is a Hopf bifurcation of the steady state, as in low-order models of the riser with the choke opening as parameter \citep{storkaas2007} and in the model problem of Section~\ref{sec:toy}. Above it, the transient from the perturbed steady state follows the pair. At an opening of 0.36 the pair predicts a growth rate of \SI{3.6e-3}{\per\second} and a period of \SI{455}{\second}; the simulation grows at \SI{2.9e-3}{\per\second} and settles on a limit cycle of \SI{450}{\second}. At 0.28, closer to the bifurcation, the period is again that of the pair (495 against \SI{518}{\second}), but the oscillation grows more slowly than predicted (\num{0.4e-3} against \SI{1.3e-3}{\per\second}) and has not saturated after \SI{6000}{\second}. From an opening of 0.70 a real global mode is unstable as well, with growth rates of \SI{0.16}{\per\second} at 0.70 that decrease to \SI{0.05}{\per\second} at full opening. Its eigenvector, concentrated in the bend and the riser, raises the void fraction there while the pressure of the line falls: gas that enters the riser lightens the column, the pressure upstream falls and more gas expands into the riser. This is the monotonic runaway on which the static criterion of \citet{taitel1986} is built, and the blowout of a severe-slugging cycle; the cycle is then a relaxation cycle, whose period is set by the filling and the blowout of the riser rather than by the pair (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}e, Section~\ref{sec:opening}).

The fast modes that remain are local. Between the openings of 0.28 and 0.36 one or two real modes of 0.6 to \SI{120}{\per\second} are concentrated at the face between the first cell of the horizontal inlet section, rich in liquid, and the stratified line; at larger openings a pair grows at about \SI{1}{\per\second} at the faces of the bend. They saturate at once: in the transients they appear as a jitter of 0.5 to \SI{3}{\percent} of the liquid flow rate at that face, without effect on the void fractions ($\pm 3\times10^{-4}$) or on the pressure.

The cell at the bottom of the bend, where the stratified pocket meets the liquid gathered at the low point, deserves a remark. With the donor face fraction it is linearly stable at the stable openings. With the mean face fraction of Section~\ref{sec:oddeven}, the steady state at 0.13 has, besides the odd--even profile of the riser (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}a), an unstable pair localised in the cells of the bend, $\lambda = 0.013 \pm 0.25\mathrm{i}\ \si{\per\second}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}b); the transient from that steady state saturates in an oscillation of the void fraction of those cells with a period of \SI{26}{\second}, the period of the pair, and a standard deviation of 0.01 to 0.02, invisible in the inlet pressure. The critical opening converges with the mesh, from 0.241 on the base mesh to 0.226 and 0.225 on the fine meshes, uniform and graded (Table~\ref{tab:hopfmesh}); above it the growth rate of the pair increases with refinement, from 3.6 to \SI{8.5e-3}{\per\second} at an opening of 0.36, and its period decreases from 455 to \SI{401}{\second}.

\subsection{Opening the choke: from stable flow to severe slugging}\label{sec:opening}

Fig.~\ref{fig:spacetime} and Table~\ref{tab:cycle} show the transients from the perturbed steady state at \SI{15}{\bar}, with outputs every second over \SI{6000}{\second}. Below the bifurcation (openings of 0.13 and 0.22) the perturbation decays and the flow pattern of the steady state persists: a stratified line, an intermittent cell at the low point, bubbly flow in the bend and the riser. Above it the oscillation of the slow pair grows into a pressure cycle. At an opening of 0.36 (Fig.~\ref{fig:spacetime}b) the riser never empties: its mean liquid fraction oscillates between 0.64 and 0.99, and the liquid that seals the line upstream of the low point lengthens from 20 to \SI{25}{\metre} and shortens again. Once per cycle a wave of gas crosses the bend, where the flow turns intermittent for a quarter of the time, and leaves the riser in a burst of gas of up to \SI{9}{\gram\per\second}, eight times the mean; the liquid outflow varies between 0.13 and \SI{0.83}{\kilogram\per\second}, 1.6 times its mean at the peak, and the inlet pressure between 5.5 and \SI{6.1}{\bar}. Waves of liquid fraction travel along the stratified line between the cycles. At an opening of 0.6 the cycle is still that of the slow pair (\SI{367}{\second}, against the \SI{315}{\second} of the pair), and still regular, with intervals of 315 to \SI{382}{\second} between blowouts, but the liquid outflow now stops briefly once per cycle and peaks at 2.3 times its mean, which classifies it as severe slugging; the riser never empties, its mean liquid fraction staying above 0.57, and its flow is bubbly or intermittent. With the choke wide open, where the real global mode is unstable, the cycle changes character (Fig.~\ref{fig:spacetime}c): the gas front reaches the riser base, the liquid column is expelled down to the bend, the top of the riser turns annular, and the liquid outflow stops for \SI{90}{\second} (median) while the riser refills, \SI{20}{\percent} of the time in all; the outflow then peaks at five times its mean in the next blowout, and the liquid that seals the line reaches \SI{46}{\metre} upstream of the low point. The cycle is irregular: complete blowouts, which bring the inlet pressure down to \SI{4.7}{\bar}, alternate with partial ones that stop at \SI{5.3}{\bar}, at intervals of 280 to \SI{660}{\second}. In both cases the gas flows back through the choke for a few seconds at the end of a blowout, at up to \SI{55}{\gram\per\second}. The mean production is that of the steady state within \SI{1}{\percent} up to an opening of 0.6 and \SI{4}{\percent} below it with the choke wide open (Fig.~\ref{fig:bifurcation}b).

\subsection{Anatomy of a cycle}\label{sec:anatomy}

Figs.~\ref{fig:anatomy} and \ref{fig:cycleprofiles} follow two cycles with the choke wide open. At the end of a blowout the inlet pressure is at its minimum, the upper half of the riser holds gas and its top is annular, and the liquid that the blowout did not carry out falls back into the bend, which turns intermittent and then bubbly. The riser refills from below in about \SI{100}{\second} while the outflow of liquid stops, and the liquid gathered at the low point seals the line: the seal grows into the descending line, up to \SI{46}{\metre} from the low point, and the gas of the line, which can no longer pass, is compressed by the inflow, raising the inlet pressure by more than \SI{1}{\bar}. Once the riser is full, liquid flows out at about \SI{0.65}{\kilogram\per\second}, more than the inflow, so that the seal shortens while the pressure of the line keeps rising slowly. When the gas front reaches the low point and the seal breaks (Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy}b), gas enters the bend as intermittent flow and the riser lightens: the pressure of the line falls by \SIrange{1.1}{1.2}{\bar} in about \SI{100}{\second}, the liquid in the riser is pushed out at up to \SI{2.5}{\kilogram\per\second}, and the gas follows it in a burst of up to \SI{20}{\gram\per\second}. The descending line stays stratified upstream of the seal, while the cells filled by the seal are bubbly (low-void rule) or intermittent (Fig.~\ref{fig:cycleprofiles}b); the dynamic identification thus follows the cycle through the four patterns without any section being prescribed. Partial cycles, in which the gas front reaches the bend but the seal re-forms before the riser empties, appear between complete ones (around \SI{5560}{\second} in Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy}).

\subsection{Stability map}\label{sec:map}

The analysis of Section~\ref{sec:linear} was repeated on the steady states of a grid of 14 choke openings and 9 reservoir pressures, obtained by continuation from the point at \SI{15}{\bar} and 0.13; 107 of the 126 points have a steady state, and the remaining 19, at reservoir pressures of \SI{30}{\bar} and above with the choke wide open, have none that the continuation reaches. Fig.~\ref{fig:map}a classifies each point by its slow global modes. The region of linear stability is bounded on the open side by the Hopf bifurcation of the slow pair up to \SI{19}{\bar}, at openings that increase from 0.07 at \SI{8}{\bar} to 0.37 at \SI{19}{\bar}, and the period of the pair at the boundary decreases from about \SI{20}{\minute} to \SI{5}{\minute}. Further from the boundary a real global mode becomes unstable as well, at the larger openings of the lower pressures. Along the boundary the pressure drop across the choke, computed from the production of the steady state with the single-phase liquid law of the choke criterion of \citet{jansen1996}, is almost constant: 0.34 to \SI{0.40}{\bar} from 10 to \SI{24}{\bar} (\SI{0.22}{\bar} at \SI{8}{\bar}). The boundary therefore has the form of that criterion, which states that the choke stabilises the flow once the pressure drop across it exceeds a critical value fixed by the geometry and the fluids, but the critical value is about half of the \SI{0.67}{\bar} that the criterion gives for this system, and the critical openings are 1.3 to 1.6 times larger (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}a). Prescribing the flow pattern by inclination instead (stratified below \SI{45}{\degree} and bubbly above, with the same low- and high-void rules) moves the crossing at \SI{15}{\bar} to an opening of 0.217, where the pair grows four times faster at 0.28 (\num{5.2e-3} against \SI{1.3e-3}{\per\second}), and gives critical pressure drops of 0.37 to \SI{0.48}{\bar} between 10 and \SI{30}{\bar}: the form of the boundary does not depend on the flow-pattern identification, its position does, by about one cell of the grid. At the most productive stable opening the inlet pressure is 6.0 to \SI{6.3}{\bar} at every reservoir pressure up to \SI{24}{\bar}, and the production is 7\,\% (at \SI{15}{\bar}) to 22\,\% (at \SI{8}{\bar}) below that of the steady state with the choke wide open, which is unstable. Above \SI{19}{\bar} the slow modes are real and the unstable region is not connected, and a fast oscillatory mode of the stratified line, with a period of 2 to \SI{3}{\second} and spread over about ten cells, is unstable at several points (stars in Fig.~\ref{fig:map}a); the line is hyperbolic at those points, so that this is a linear instability of the stratified layer rather than a loss of well-posedness.

\section{Practical recommendations}\label{sec:lessons}

The difficulties met along the way are common to transient two-fluid codes; the following recommendations summarise what was measured.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A numerical method that does not require hyperbolicity does not make an ill-posed model usable: ill-posedness appears as growth that increases with mesh refinement. Check the characteristics on the states the simulation visits, and regularise with physical terms where it goes.
\item Collocated schemes without dissipation on the void waves, and backward differences applied to all variables, cannot carry the fronts of a severe-slugging cycle; a staggered scheme with phase-wise donor cells can.
\item Use the same void fraction in every term of a face momentum balance; mixing the donor value with the mean creates an odd--even mode.
\item Do not limit the void fraction to keep it in bounds; limit the fluxes of the phase that the donor cell does not have, and relax the vanishing phase towards no slip, not towards rest.
\item Integrate conservative variables. Verify the mass balance with the boundary fluxes integrated by the same recurrence as the states, never by quadrature of the outputs.
\item Flow-pattern criteria built for steady flow must be evaluated on the local state, with smooth switches of relative width and weights that form a partition of unity, before they can be used in a transient implicit solver.
\item Closure terms that are dimensionally inconsistent may compensate each other at one scale and fail at another; check them at more than one pressure and diameter.
\item A choke law with a mass-weighted mixture density does not resist gas and is singular in reverse flow; use the no-slip volume-weighted density.
\item A closed-box integrator prevents conservative residuals, projection on physical bounds and range-scaled tolerances, all of which were needed; an exact Jacobian does not reduce Newton iterations when the closures are not smooth, but compiling the right-hand side reduces the cost of each one.
\item When a transient starts on, or settles near, an equilibrium, bound the time step: an implicit multistep formula with steps much longer than the time scale of an instability damps it, and the run reports a stable state that is not. Even then, a slow instability may not grow enough within a simulated window; decide the stability of an equilibrium from its eigenvalues.
\item Linearise a conservative formulation with its mass matrix, $\bJ\bv = \lambda\bM\bv$. The eigenvalues of $\bJ$ alone show fast unstable modes that do not exist and hide the slow pair whose crossing of the imaginary axis marks the onset of the cycles; with the mass matrix the steady state predicts the onset and the period of the cycles near it, and transient simulations are needed for the amplitude of the cycles and where they coexist with a stable steady state.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

A four-equation two-fluid model with the flow pattern identified dynamically along the whole system simulates severe slugging in a well--pipeline--riser system once five conditions are met. The closures must be regularised where the simulation goes: with a low-void rule and an objective virtual-mass term the model is hyperbolic in all but \tbd{X}\,\% of the states that a severe-slugging cycle visits. The discretisation must be staggered, with phase-wise donor cells, donor face closures and a donor-cell limiter of the mass fluxes: collocated schemes do not reach the first blowout, and mean face closures create an odd--even steady state and an unstable oscillation of the cells at the bend. The time integration must conserve the phase masses: the primitive form loses \SI{0.08}{\percent} of the liquid and creates \SI{0.45}{\percent} of the gas in \SI{3000}{\second} and changes the cycles. The integrator must project the states on their bounds, scale its tolerances with the ranges of the states and bound its step near an equilibrium. And the flow-pattern criteria must be evaluated on the local state with smooth switches. A compiled right-hand side with exact Jacobians makes each step \tbd{X} times cheaper and the maps of hundreds of long transients affordable.

The linearisation of the conservative formulation requires its mass matrix. With it, the steady state of the discretised system predicts the onset of the cycles: a slow complex pair, which exchanges liquid between the riser and the gas pocket of the line, crosses the imaginary axis at a choke opening that grows with the reservoir pressure, and its period is that of the simulated cycles near the onset; further from it a real global mode, the static runaway of the riser, becomes unstable and the cycle becomes a relaxation cycle with complete blockage of the riser. The boundary follows a constant pressure drop across the choke, the form of the criterion of \citet{jansen1996}, but at about \SI{0.36}{\bar} instead of the \SI{0.67}{\bar} of that criterion, and the most productive stable opening keeps the inlet pressure at 6.0 to \SI{6.3}{\bar} whatever the reservoir pressure. \tbd{(Transient map from a blocked line: agreement of the classes with the linear ones, bistability.)}

\section{Closure relations}\label{app:closures}

Wall and interfacial shear stresses are written $\tau = f\rho v|v|/2$ with Fanning factors from \citet{churchill1977} on the hydraulic diameter of each phase, and $|v|$ regularised as $(v^2 + \epsilon^2)^{1/2}$ with $\epsilon = \SI{e-4}{\metre\per\second}$ so that the stresses are smooth at rest. The forces per unit length in Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentum} are $\Gamma_L = \tau_{wL}S_L - \tau_i S_i$ and $\Gamma_G = \tau_{wG}S_G + \tau_i S_i$.

\paragraph{Stratified flow} The wetted angle $\beta$ follows from $\beta - \sin\beta = 2\pi\aL$; the perimeters are $S_L = \beta D/2$, $S_G = \pi D - S_L$ and $S_i = D\sin(\beta/2)$, and the level $h_L = D[1 - \cos(\beta/2)]/2$. The interfacial friction factor is $f_i = f_G\,[1 + 15\,(h_L/D)^{1/2}\max(0, v_{GS}/v_{GT} - 1)]$ with $v_{GT} = \SI{5.5}{\metre\per\second}$ \citep{andritsos1987}. The pressure corrections are the hydrostatic heads between the interface and the centroids of the two phase areas, $z_L$ and $z_G$ measured from the pipe bottom,
\begin{equation}
\Delta P_L = \aG\,\Delta P_{LG}, \quad \Delta P_G = -\aL\,\Delta P_{LG}, \quad \Delta P_{LG} = \left[(\rho_L - \rho_G)h_L - \rho_L z_L + \rho_G z_G\right] g\cos\theta,
\end{equation}
whose derivative with respect to $\aG$ is the level-gradient term that keeps stratified flow hyperbolic.

\paragraph{Annular flow} The film thickness is $\delta = D(1 - \aG^{1/2})/2$ and the interfacial perimeter $S_i = \pi D\aG^{1/2}$; the film friction follows \citet{kowalski1987}, $f_L = \max[24/Re_L, 0.263\,\aL^{-1}(D_L/(Re_L D))^{1/2}]$, and the interfacial friction the stratified expression with $h_L = \delta$. Both phases are at the mixture pressure.

\paragraph{Bubbly flow} The bubble diameter is the smaller of $d_e = (16\sigma/g\Delta\rho)^{1/2}$ and the breakup diameter $d_{max} = [0.725 + 4.15(v_{GS}/v_M)^{1/2}](\sigma/\rho_L)^{3/5}(2 f_M v_M^3/D)^{-2/5}$ \citep{hinze1955,calderbank1958}. The terminal velocity $v_b = (2.14\sigma/\rho_L d_b + 0.505 g d_b)^{1/2}$ \citep{mendelson1967,fan1990} defines the drag coefficient $C_D = 4 g d_b\Delta\rho/(3\rho_L v_b^2)$, and the interfacial force per unit length is $\tfrac34 C_D \aG\rho_L A\,|v_r|v_r/d_b$ with $v_r = v_G - v_L$, so that drag balances buoyancy at the terminal velocity. The liquid pressure correction is $\Delta P_L = 0.25\rho_L v_r^2$ \citep{stuhmiller1977}, with $|v_r|$ limited to \SI{3}{\metre\per\second}.

\paragraph{Intermittent flow} A slug unit of length $l_u = v_t/\omega_s$ consists of a liquid slug of length $l_s$ and holdup $R_s$, treated with the bubbly closure, and a film region of length $l_f = l_u - l_s$ treated with the stratified closure; its terms are the length-weighted averages of the two. The translational velocity is $v_t = 1.2 v_M + (0.35\sin\theta + 0.54\cos\theta)(gD)^{1/2}$ \citep{bendiksen1984}, the slug holdup follows \citet{barnea1985}, the slug length \citet{nicholson1978} and the slug frequency $\omega_s$ \citet{zabaras2000}; the film holdup and the local velocities follow from the mass balances of the unit.
