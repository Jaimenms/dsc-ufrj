\begin{abstract}
% <= 250 palavras, autocontido, sem referências.
Severe slugging in pipeline--riser systems combines phase disappearance, moving liquid and gas fronts, changes of flow pattern and cycles of hundreds of seconds, which makes it a demanding test for transient two-fluid simulators. This work asks what a one-dimensional, four-equation two-fluid model with pattern-dependent closures needs to reproduce the cycle when the flow pattern is identified dynamically along the whole system, and uses the answer on a well--flowline--riser system of field dimensions (a \SI{1500}{\metre} well, a \SI{2}{\kilo\metre} flowline and a \SI{317}{\metre} riser) with a topside choke. A characteristic analysis shows that the closures are ill-posed in large parts of their domain; a low-void rule, an objective virtual-mass term and an interfacial pressure in the slug unit leave the model hyperbolic in all but \SI{0.1}{\percent} of the states visited by a severe-slugging simulation, against \SI{29}{\percent} without the virtual mass. The slug unit of the intermittent closure must take its film from the physics of the film rather than from the mass balance of the cell, or the unit is sold short at the triple point where the slug, the film and the cell hold the same gas, and the bubbly criteria of the pattern identification must read the void fraction of the cell rather than the gas flow rate. Only a staggered finite-volume scheme with phase-wise donor cells carries the cycle; integrating the non-conservative form loses liquid at every blowout, whereas a conservative residual closes the mass balances to the Newton tolerance, and evaluating the face closures with the donor void fraction removes an odd--even mode and an unstable oscillation of the cells at the bend. A variable-step implicit integrator with physical bounds, range-scaled tolerances, a line search that stops at convergence and a second-order error estimate, driven by a compiled right-hand side with exact Jacobians, runs four hours of slugging in four to eight minutes. Linearised with the mass matrix of the conservative formulation, the steady state loses stability through two complex pairs --- the riser column oscillating against the gas pocket, and the exchange of liquid between flowline and riser, whose period is that of the simulated cycles --- and, with the choke open, through a real mode: the onset of slugging is a Hopf bifurcation, located at a choke opening where the pressure drop across the choke is \SI{3.7}{\bar} against a riser head of \SI{20}{\bar}, as the criterion of Jansen and co-workers predicts. With the choke open the field case settles on a period-two cycle, with blowouts of 33 and \SI{45}{\kilogram\per\second} against a mean production of \SI{3.9}{\kilogram\per\second} and the flowline pressure swinging between 15 and \SI{31}{\bar}; closing the choke to 0.3 leaves a pressure cycle with liquid pulses, closing it to 0.1 stabilises the riser at the cost of \SI{7}{\percent} of the production, and the gas--oil ratio of the well sets the period of the cycle but neither its pressure range nor its surge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Severe slugging is the cyclic blockage and blowout of a riser by liquid that accumulates at a low point of a production line \citep{schmidt1980,boe1981,pots1987}. When the gas in the line cannot carry the liquid up the riser, the liquid seals the riser base, the line pressurises while the riser fills, and once the pressure in the line overcomes the hydrostatic head the gas penetrates the riser and expels the liquid in a short burst, after which the liquid falls back and the cycle restarts \citep{taitel1986,fabre1990,taitel1990a}. The cycle loads separators with liquid surges, causes pressure oscillations along the system and reduces production. Choking at the top of the riser can stabilise the flow \citep{schmidt1979,jansen1996}, at the price of a back-pressure, which makes the stability boundary in the plane of choke opening and inflow conditions the central question for operation.

That boundary is a question of the stability of a steady state, and a few terms of the theory of dynamical systems will be used throughout \citep{strogatz2024,kuznetsov2004}. A steady state is \emph{stable} if every small perturbation of it decays. Linearised about the steady state, a perturbation is a sum of modes that evolve as $e^{\lambda t}$, where the $\lambda$ are the eigenvalues of the linearised system: the real part of an eigenvalue is the growth rate of its mode, the imaginary part its angular frequency, and the steady state is stable if all real parts are negative. As a parameter of the system changes --- here the choke opening --- the steady state can lose its stability where an eigenvalue crosses the imaginary axis; such a parameter value is a \emph{bifurcation}. In a \emph{Hopf bifurcation} the crossing is made by a pair of complex-conjugate eigenvalues, $\lambda = \sigma \pm \mathrm{i}\,\omega$ with $\sigma$ changing sign and $\omega \neq 0$: beyond it a perturbation grows as an oscillation of period $2\pi/\omega$, until the non-linear terms bound it in a periodic oscillation of finite amplitude, a \emph{limit cycle}, whose period near the bifurcation is $2\pi/\omega$. When the limit cycle grows from zero amplitude at the bifurcation (a supercritical bifurcation), the transition is gradual; when a cycle of finite amplitude already exists before it (subcritical), the stable steady state and the cycle coexist over a range of the parameter, and which one the system reaches depends on where it starts --- a \emph{hysteresis}. Further from the bifurcation the two eigenvalues of the pair may meet on the real axis and become real and positive. The oscillation is then a \emph{relaxation oscillation} \citep{vanderpol1926}: a slow accumulation followed by a fast discharge, whose period is set by the accumulation rather than by an eigenvalue. A severe-slugging cycle, with its slow pressurisation of the line and its blowout, has this shape. Section~\ref{sec:toy} illustrates each of these terms on a model problem.

Models of severe slugging range from lumped descriptions of the cycle \citep{taitel1990a,sarica1991} and low-order models for control \citep{storkaas2007,jahanshahi2011}, through drift-flux formulations and their nonlinear stability analysis \citep{balino2010,azevedo2023}, to one-dimensional two-fluid models of the kind used in commercial transient simulators \citep{bendiksen1991,pauchon1994,masella1998}, which have been compared with experiments in pipeline--riser systems and with choking \citep{malekzadeh2012,park2014,li2022}, to slug-capturing and slug-tracking studies \citep{issa2003,bonizzi2009,kjeldby2013,smith2016}. \citet{han2015} simulated terrain-induced severe slugging with a two-fluid model whose riser equations were made well-posed by an interfacial-pressure term, while the horizontal and descending sections were kept within the well-posed region of their closures. The two-fluid model is the natural framework, because it represents the slip between the phases, the fronts and the flow-pattern changes that a cycle involves, but it brings well-known difficulties: the model is not hyperbolic for every state and closure \citep{stuhmiller1977,ramshaw1978,lopezdebertodano2017}, phases appear and disappear, the closures change with the flow pattern, and the dynamics combine acoustic and void-wave time scales with a cycle of hundreds of seconds. Commercial codes resolve these difficulties in ways that are not published in full, and open studies usually prescribe the flow pattern of each section of the system.

This article reports what was needed to make a four-equation two-fluid model with pattern-dependent closures \citep{souza2010} simulate severe slugging with the flow pattern identified dynamically along the whole system, from the well inflow to the separator, and what can then be learnt from it about the stability of a well--pipeline--riser system with a topside choke. Section~\ref{sec:toy} first presents the method of analysis on a model problem with two linear equations and a single nonlinear boundary condition. The presentation then follows the chain of decisions in the order in which they matter: the model and its well-posedness (Sections~\ref{sec:model} and \ref{sec:wellposed}), the spatial discretisation (Section~\ref{sec:space}), the time integration and what happens when its ingredients are removed (Section~\ref{sec:time}), the compiled backend (Section~\ref{sec:backend}), and the application to a well--pipeline--riser system (Section~\ref{sec:application}). The contributions are:
(i) a dynamic flow-pattern identification, smooth and evaluated on the local state, that can be used inside an implicit transient solver;
(ii) a characteristic analysis by flow pattern, a regularisation built from physical terms, and a measure of well-posedness along the simulated trajectories rather than on a map;
(iii) a comparison of collocated and staggered discretisations and of primitive and conservative time integration on the same severe-slugging problem, with exact mass balances, and the identification of an odd--even mode created by inconsistent face void fractions;
(iv) an implicit integrator and a compiled right-hand side with exact Jacobians that make long transients of a well--flowline--riser system affordable;
(v) the linear stability of the steady state of the discretised system, with the mass matrix that a conservative formulation requires, which predicts the onset and the period of the cycles;
(vi) profiles, fronts and flow patterns along the cycle in a well--flowline--riser system of field dimensions, the stabilisation by the choke and the effect of the gas--oil ratio of the well.
Section~\ref{sec:lessons} collects the practical recommendations.

\section{A model problem}\label{sec:toy}

The analysis that this article applies to severe slugging --- a semi-discretisation in conservative variables, the linearisation of the steady state with its mass matrix, a continuation in the choke opening and transient simulations that test the linear predictions --- can be followed step by step on a much simpler problem with the same structure. A line $0 < x < 1$ carries a pressure $p$ and a flux $w$ governed by the telegraph equations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toyline}
C\pd{p}{t} + \pd{w}{x} = 0, \qquad L\pd{w}{t} + \pd{p}{x} = -R\,w,
\end{equation}
two linear partial differential equations with constant coefficients, hyperbolic with wave speeds $\pm 1/\sqrt{LC}$. The line is fed at $x = 0$ by a linear source, $w(0,t) = k\,(P_r - p(0,t))$, and closed at $x = 1$ by an element with a single state $y$, the flux through it,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toyend}
m\,\frac{\dd y}{\dd t} = p(1,t) - f(y) - a\,y, \qquad w(1,t) = y, \qquad f(y) = g_0 - \beta\,(y - y_0) + \tfrac{1}{3}(y - y_0)^3 .
\end{equation}
The line stands for the pipeline and its gas pocket, the source for the inflow from the reservoir, the end element for the riser and the resistance $a$ for the choke. The characteristic $f$ of the end element falls with the flux around $y_0$, as the head of a riser that lightens when more gas flows through it, and the cubic term bounds it; it is the only nonlinearity of the problem. A transmission line terminated by such a negative-resistance element is the tunnel-diode line of \citet{nagumo1961}, a close relative of the equations of \citet{fitzhugh1961} and \citet{nagumo1962}, and its oscillations are those of \citet{vanderpol1926}.

The line is discretised as the two-fluid model will be (Section~\ref{sec:space}): $p$ at the centres of 50 cells, $w$ at the faces, the inlet face given by the source and the outlet face by $y$. The semi-discrete right-hand side returns the time derivatives of the charges $C p$, of the momenta $L w$ and of $m y$, as the conservative formulation of the two-fluid model returns those of the phase masses, so that the linearisation about a steady state is the generalised eigenvalue problem $\bJ\bv = \lambda\bM\bv$ with $\bM = \mathrm{diag}(C, L, m)$. A lumped approximation shows what to expect. If the inertance and the friction of the line are neglected, the pressure is uniform along it, $p(x,t) \approx P(t)$, and integrating the first of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:toyline} over the line leaves two ordinary differential equations, for $P$ and for the flux $y$ through the end element:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toylumpedode}
C\,\frac{\dd P}{\dd t} = k\,(P_r - P) - y, \qquad m\,\frac{\dd y}{\dd t} = P - f(y) - a\,y .
\end{equation}
At the steady state $(P^*, y^*)$ the inflow equals the outflow, $k\,(P_r - P^*) = y^*$, and the pressure balances the end element, $P^* = f(y^*) + a\,y^*$. Small deviations $\delta P$ and $\delta y$ from it obey
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toylumped}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix}\delta P \\ \delta y\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{B}\begin{pmatrix}\delta P \\ \delta y\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix} -k/C & -1/C \\ 1/m & -r/m \end{pmatrix}, \qquad r = a + f'(y^*),
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the net differential resistance of the end element: the resistance $a$ of the `choke' plus the slope of the `riser' characteristic, which is negative near $y_0$. The factors $1/C$ and $1/m$ are the inverse of the mass matrix of this two-state system, so that $\mathbf{B}$ is its $\bM^{-1}\bJ$. The steady state is stable while the trace of $\mathbf{B}$, $-k/C - r/m$, is negative and its determinant, $(1 + k\,r)/(Cm)$, is positive. As $a$ decreases, $r$ decreases, and the trace vanishes at $r = -mk/C$: a pair of complex eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis, a Hopf bifurcation in which the end element oscillates against the capacitance of the line with the frequency $\sqrt{\det\mathbf{B}} \approx 1/\sqrt{Cm}$. The two eigenvalues become real when the discriminant of $\mathbf{B}$, $(k/C - r/m)^2 - 4/(Cm)$, vanishes, at $r = mk/C - 2\sqrt{m/C}$; between the two values of $r$ the steady state is an unstable focus, below the second an unstable node.

Fig.~\ref{fig:toy} follows the problem with $C = 10$, $L = R = 0.05$, $k = 0.5$, $m = 0.1$, $\beta = g_0 = y_0 = 1$ and $P_r$ such that $y^* = 1$ at $a = 1$. A large resistance stabilises the line. As $a$ decreases, the slow pair crosses the imaginary axis at $a_H = 0.976$ with a period of 6.75 (the lumped approximation gives 0.995 and 6.29), and its two eigenvalues meet on the real axis at $a_R = 0.756$ (0.792), below which two real global modes are unstable (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}b). The transients, integrated with the implicit formula of Section~\ref{sec:time} from the steady state perturbed by \SI{0.1}{\percent}, follow the spectrum. Above $a_H$ the perturbation decays. Just below, it grows into a nearly sinusoidal cycle with the period of the pair (6.8 at $a = 0.95$, against 6.83 predicted), which widens as $a$ decreases (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}a, d). Below $a_R$ the cycle becomes a relaxation cycle: the line charges slowly while the flux through the end element stays on the left branch of $f$, the flux jumps to the right branch and the line discharges, with a flux wave that runs from the end into the line (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}e), and the flux jumps back, overshooting to negative values. Its period, 12.3 at $a = 0.5$, is set by the charging of the line, not by the pair, which no longer exists. In the order in which the choke opens, these are the stable flow, the pressure cycle and the severe slugging of Section~\ref{sec:application}, including the backflow through the choke at the end of a blowout.

The mass matrix matters. The eigenvalues of $\bJ$ alone, the Jacobian of the time derivatives of the charges and momenta, are those of a system in which $C$, $L$ and $m$ were all set to one: their largest real part stays at $-0.025$ for every $a$, and the line would be declared stable at every opening (Fig.~\ref{fig:toy}b).

What the model problem does not have is the rest of this article: the characteristics of the two-fluid model can become complex, phases vanish, the masses are non-linear functions of the state (with a constant capacitance the primitive and conservative formulations coincide; with one that depends on the pressure, as for a gas, they do not), and the closures change with the flow pattern. The following sections take these up in the order of the introduction.

\section{Mathematical model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two-fluid equations}\label{sec:equations}

The gas ($k = G$) and liquid ($k = L$) phases flow in a pipe of constant cross-section $A$ and inclination $\theta(x)$. With the area fractions $\aG + \aL = 1$, the phase mass flow rates $q_k = \rho_k \alpha_k A v_k$ and a common pressure $P$, the mass and momentum balances are
\begin{gather}
\pd{}{t}\left(\rho_k \alpha_k A\right) + \pd{q_k}{x} = 0, \label{eq:mass}\\
\pd{q_k}{t} + \pd{}{x}\left(q_k v_k\right) = -\alpha_k A \pd{P}{x} - A\pd{}{x}\left(\alpha_k \Delta P_k\right) - \Gamma_k - \rho_k \alpha_k A g \sin\theta + F^{vm}_k, \label{eq:momentum}
\end{gather}
where $\Delta P_k$ is the difference between the average pressure of phase $k$ and $P$ (the hydrostatic level gradient in stratified flow, the interfacial pressure of dispersed flow), $\Gamma_k$ the wall and interfacial shear forces per unit length and $F^{vm}_k$ the virtual-mass force. Each phase follows a polytropic law, $\rho_k = \rho_{k0}(P/P_0)^{1/\gamma_k}$, with $\gamma_G$ close to one for the gas and a large $\gamma_L$ for the liquid. For the analysis the system is written in the primitive variables $\by = (\aG, P, q_L, q_G)$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quasilinear}
\pd{\by}{t} + \bA(\by)\,\pd{\by}{x} + \mathbf{b}(\by) = \mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
whose matrix $\bA$ contains the convective terms, the pressure coupling through the phase compressibilities and the derivatives of $\Delta P_k$ with respect to $\aG$ and $P$. The virtual-mass force acts in dispersed bubbly flow and is written in the objective form of \citet{drew1979},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vm}
F^{vm}_G = -F^{vm}_L = -C_{vm}\, w_B\, \aG \rho_L A \left[\frac{D_G v_G}{Dt} - \frac{D_L v_L}{Dt} + (v_G - v_L)\left((\lambda - 2)\pd{v_G}{x} + (1 - \lambda)\pd{v_L}{x}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
with $C_{vm} = 0.5$, $\lambda = 1$ and $w_B$ the weight of the bubbly pattern (Section~\ref{sec:patterns}).

\subsection{Closure relations}\label{sec:closures}

The closures follow the flow pattern. In \emph{stratified} flow the wetted perimeters and the hydrostatic pressure corrections follow from the liquid level \citep{taitel1976}, the wall friction from the Fanning factor of \citet{churchill1977} on each phase's hydraulic diameter, and the interfacial friction from \citet{andritsos1987}. In \emph{annular} flow the film friction follows \citet{kowalski1987} and the interfacial friction the same correlation on the film thickness. In \emph{bubbly} flow the bubble diameter is limited by the Hinze--Calderbank breakup diameter \citep{hinze1955}, the terminal velocity follows \citet{mendelson1967} and \citet{fan1990}, the interfacial drag balances buoyancy at the terminal velocity, and the interfacial pressure is $\Delta P_L = 0.25\rho_L (v_G - v_L)^2$ \citep{stuhmiller1977}. In \emph{intermittent} flow a slug unit made of an aerated liquid slug and a stratified film region \citep{taitel1990b} weighs the bubbly and stratified closures by the lengths of the two regions, with the slug-body holdup of \citet{barnea1985}, the translational velocity of \citet{bendiksen1984} and the slug frequency of \citet{zabaras2000}. The film region takes its gas fraction from the physics of the film, the stratified equilibrium of \citet{taitel1976} in horizontal and downward sections and a Taylor bubble with a falling film \citep{taitel1980} in upward ones, and the slug fraction of the unit follows from the gas content of the cell, so that the unit always conserves the mass of the cell and reduces to the state of the cell in the liquid-rich and gas-rich limits; the slug frequency enters through the acceleration loss of the film picked up by the slug front \citep{taitel1990b}. Appendix~\ref{app:closures} gives the expressions. Where a phase vanishes, its velocity is undefined; Section~\ref{sec:space} treats this numerically.

\subsection{Dynamic flow-pattern identification}\label{sec:patterns}

The flow pattern is identified at every cell and every time from stability criteria: the Kelvin--Helmholtz criterion for the stability of stratified flow in the direction of the pipe and the liquid-film trajectory criterion for reverse flow \citep{taitel1976,barnea1982}, wind-generated waves for the wavy subpattern, the film-bridging and Froude criteria for annular flow \citep{taitel1980,barnea1987}, and the dispersion and coalescence criteria that separate bubbly from intermittent flow \citep{taitel1980,barnea1987}. The criteria, listed in Appendix~\ref{app:patterns}, are combined into weights $w_S$, $w_A$, $w_B$, $w_I$ of the stratified, annular, bubbly and intermittent closures,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weights}
w_A = (1 - w_S)\,p_A, \qquad w_B = (1 - w_S)(1 - w_A)\,p_B, \qquad w_I = (1 - w_S)(1 - w_A)(1 - p_B),
\end{equation}
so that $w_S + w_A + w_B + w_I = 1$ for any value of the indicators. The closure terms are the weighted sums of the four closures.

Steady-state flow-pattern maps compare superficial velocities with equilibrium solutions of the stratified and annular momentum balances; used as they are inside a transient solver they fail in three ways, which the present identification avoids. First, a sharp switch of the closures makes the residual non-smooth, and a Newton iteration then alternates between patterns from one iteration to the next; the indicators are therefore logistic functions whose width is a fraction of the critical value, $1/(1 + e^{-k (X - X_c)/|X_c|})$ with $k = 20$, so that criteria of very different magnitudes switch over the same relative interval. Second, the equilibrium level computed from superficial velocities does not describe a cell whose content is far from equilibrium: a gas pocket at rest in front of a liquid column has small superficial velocities and would be classified as bubbly flow. The criteria are therefore evaluated on the local state: the actual level and void fraction replace the equilibrium ones in the stratified criteria, the bubbly criteria (the packing limits of 0.25 and 0.52) use the void fraction of the cell itself and not an estimate from the gas flow rate --- a cell with two per cent of gas at the riser bend would otherwise follow the gas flow rate into the intermittent closures, whose drag is eighteen times smaller, and its effective drag would turn negative, the film-bridging criterion of annular flow uses the larger of the equilibrium film fraction and the liquid fraction of the cell (a cell that holds more liquid than a bridging film cannot be annular, however small the superficial velocities), stratified flow is excluded in vertical sections and annular flow is assigned above $\aG = 0.9$. Third, when the gas fraction is very small no pattern can be identified, since the criteria degenerate; below $\aG = 0.025$ the closures blend smoothly into the bubbly ones (low-void rule; Section~\ref{sec:wellposed}).

\subsection{Well inflow, choke and separator}\label{sec:bc}

The well receives the reservoir fluids through a linear inflow performance relation, $q_k = J_k\max(0, P_{res} - P_{wf})$, with $J_G/J_L$ the free-gas to liquid mass ratio of the produced fluid (Section~\ref{sec:case}), and the riser discharges through a choke into a separator at fixed pressure; the well and the flowline are two pipes of different diameters joined at the wellhead, where the flow rates of the well are imposed on the flowline inlet and the pressure of the flowline on the well outlet. The choke follows a homogeneous law: the mixture crosses the valve with the no-slip density weighted by volume, $\Delta P = \dot m\, Q/(K a)^2$, with $\dot m$ and $Q$ the mass and volumetric flow rates, $a \in (0, 1]$ the opening and $K a$ an effective flow area; the densities are evaluated at the downstream pressure. In the units of the simulator ($\Delta P$ in bar, $\dot m$ in \si{\kilogram\per\second}, $Q$ in \si{\cubic\metre\per\second}) $K = 0.0075\,c_v$ with the valve coefficient $c_v = 150$, which in SI units is an area of \SI{35.6}{\square\centi\metre} for the fully open choke, against \SI{182}{\square\centi\metre} for the pipe. A density weighted by mass flow, $\rho_M = (q_L\rho_L + q_G\rho_G)/(q_L + q_G)$, which is sometimes used, makes the choke almost transparent to gas, because the gas carries little mass, and becomes singular when the gas flows back through the choke at the end of a blowout and $q_L + q_G$ crosses zero; with it the choke does not stabilise the riser (Section~\ref{sec:opening}).

\section{Well-posedness}\label{sec:wellposed}

\subsection{Characteristic analysis by flow pattern}\label{sec:characteristics}

The system~\eqref{eq:quasilinear} is hyperbolic when the eigenvalues of $\bA$ are real; complex eigenvalues make the initial-value problem ill-posed, with growth rates proportional to the wavenumber, so that the numerical solution does not converge as the mesh is refined \citep{lopezdebertodano2017}. Fig.~\ref{fig:hyperbolicity} maps the largest imaginary part of the characteristic velocities over void fraction and slip for each closure at the conditions of the flowline of the application (\SI{20}{\bar}, $D = \SI{152}{\milli\metre}$, $v_L = \SI{0.5}{\metre\per\second}$), and Table~\ref{tab:hyperbolicity} gives the ill-posed fractions. Four regions are ill-posed. The stratified closure loses hyperbolicity when the gas layer becomes very thin, $\aG \lesssim 0.005$, for any slip: the hydrostatic level gradient, which makes stratified flow hyperbolic up to the inviscid Kelvin--Helmholtz limit \citep{barnea1994}, vanishes when the level reaches the top of the pipe. This is precisely the state in which every liquid-filled cell is evaluated, since the void fraction of a cell without gas is cut at a small positive value. The bubbly closure is ill-posed at low void fraction with slip, the classical result of \citet{stuhmiller1977}. The annular closure, with both phases at the same pressure, is ill-posed for almost any slip, and the intermittent closure at low void fraction and at large slip.

\subsection{Regularisation}\label{sec:regularisation}

Three terms remove the ill-posed regions without changing the equations elsewhere. The low-void rule blends the closures of any pattern into the bubbly ones below $\aG = 0.025$ with a $C^1$ ramp, which removes the thin-layer limit of the stratified closure; physically, a stratified gas layer of one per cent of the cross-section is not a flow pattern. The objective virtual-mass term \eqref{eq:vm} makes bubbly flow hyperbolic for every slip \citep{drew1979}; with $\lambda = 2$, which keeps only the difference of material accelerations, the bubbly map becomes \emph{more} ill-posed (\SI{99}{\percent} of the map against \SI{46}{\percent} without virtual mass), so the objective terms are essential. An interfacial-pressure term of the form proposed by \citet{bestion1990}, $\Delta P_i = \delta\, \aG \aL \rho_G \rho_L (v_G - v_L)^2/(\aG\rho_L + \aL\rho_G)$ with $\delta \geq 1$, makes the annular and intermittent closures hyperbolic (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyperbolicity}d, h), but has no physical basis in those patterns and does not act on the thin stratified layer, where it is proportional to $\aG$. Surface tension adds a third-order dispersive term that limits the growth of capillary waves at millimetre scale; it does not change the characteristics and is irrelevant at cell sizes of metres.

\subsection{Well-posedness along the simulated trajectories}\label{sec:trajectories}

A map shows where a closure is ill-posed, not whether a simulation goes there. The eigenvalues of $\bA$ were therefore evaluated in every cell and at every output time of the simulations of Section~\ref{sec:application}. For the severe-slugging run with the choke open (\SI{16000}{\second}, one state every \SI{5}{\second}), \SI{0.1}{\percent} of the cell--time states are ill-posed with the low-void rule and the virtual mass. They are intermittent or annular cells (\SI{0.3}{\percent} and \SI{0.2}{\percent} of the states with those patterns, and none of the stratified or bubbly ones), in the flowline and at the bend during the blowouts; none is in the riser. Evaluated on the same states without the virtual mass, the fraction rises to \SI{29}{\percent}, with three quarters of the bubbly cells and \SI{40}{\percent} of the riser states ill-posed, and without the low-void rule to \SI{4.3}{\percent}, a tenth of the cells with little gas (Table~\ref{tab:trajectory}). The interfacial-pressure term of \citet{bestion1990} would leave none (last row of Table~\ref{tab:trajectory}); since it has no physical basis in the annular and intermittent patterns, it is not used in what follows. Without the virtual mass the simulation still completes the same cycle, with 1.6 times the steps (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}): at this mesh the ill-posed states cost steps, not the cycle, because the growth they produce is bounded by the cell size and damped by the implicit scheme.

\section{Spatial discretisation}\label{sec:space}

\subsection{Schemes}\label{sec:schemes}

Five discretisations were applied to the same problem. Four are collocated, with the four primitive variables at the same nodes: the Galerkin and Petrov--Galerkin finite-element methods, and central and backward finite differences, the latter applied to all variables, the pressure included. The fifth is a staggered finite-volume scheme in the spirit of \citet{harlow1965} and \citet{patankar1980}: void fraction and pressure at the cell centres, phase mass flow rates at the faces. The mass fluxes and the momentum flux of each phase use the donor cell defined by the sign of that phase's flow rate, the pressure gradient is centred between neighbouring cells, and the pressure corrections $\Delta P_k$ are differenced between cell centres.

\subsection{What a severe-slugging cycle demands}\label{sec:demands}

A cycle requires that a front between a liquid column and a gas pocket travel through the bend and up the riser, that a phase vanish from a cell and reappear, and that the inventory of both phases be conserved over many cycles. The collocated schemes of Section~\ref{sec:schemes} have no dissipation on the void waves and develop node-to-node oscillations at the fronts; the backward difference applied to the pressure gradient of the primitive form is not conservative, and the Galerkin schemes fail at the first front, where the Newton iterations of the time step stop converging within a few seconds of simulated time. Only the staggered scheme completes the cycles, and only its conservative time integration (Section~\ref{sec:conservation}) keeps the inventories; Table~\ref{tab:ablation} compares the two time formulations on the application case. On the offshore case the primitive integration of the same staggered scheme creates \SI{0.5}{\percent} of the liquid fed and loses \SI{0.07}{\percent} of the gas over five cycles (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\subsection{Vanishing phases}\label{sec:vanishing}

In a liquid-filled cell the gas momentum equation has no mass and the gas flow rate is undetermined. Three choices proved necessary. The mass flux of a phase through a face is multiplied by a $C^1$ limiter of the donor cell's fraction of that phase, so that a cell without a phase cannot give it away; this keeps the inventories non-negative without limiting the void fraction itself. A limiter on the void fraction, the obvious alternative, destroys mass whenever it acts and produces cycles that are artefacts of the lost liquid. The flow rate of the vanishing phase is relaxed, with a time constant of \SI{0.05}{\second}, to the value it would have without slip, computed with the donor cell's fraction floored at the small value at which the closures see the phase. Relaxing it to a zero flow rate instead leaves a virtual gas layer at rest above the moving liquid; a stratified closure evaluated in that cell sees a gas layer sheared by the liquid and exerts on a liquid-filled pipe an interfacial friction tens of times the wall friction, which slows a liquid column down. With the low-void rule of Section~\ref{sec:regularisation} the cell is evaluated with the bubbly closures and the two targets give the same cycle (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}); the no-slip target remains the one that does not depend on that rule. Finally, an artificial viscosity $\nu = C c h$ in the momentum equations, with $c$ the phase sound speed, $h$ the cell size and $C = 0.05$, damps the acoustic ringing of the short liquid columns that form at the riser base after a blowout, which otherwise forces time steps of milliseconds; it vanishes as $h \to 0$ and does not affect waves much longer than a cell.

\subsection{Face void fraction and an odd--even mode}\label{sec:oddeven}

In the momentum equation of a face, the area fraction that multiplies the pressure gradient and the gravity term is that of the donor cell, while the closures (wall and interfacial friction) are naturally evaluated with the state at the face. If that state takes the arithmetic mean of the void fractions of the two neighbouring cells, the steady momentum balance of each face relates the donor fraction to the mean one; with the donor fraction $\alpha_i$ given, the balance fixes $(\alpha_i + \alpha_{i+1})/2$, so that $\alpha_{i+1} \approx 2\alpha^\ast - \alpha_i$ and the discrete steady state admits an odd--even mode. In the riser the void fraction then alternates from cell to cell with an amplitude that grows upwards. Evaluating the face closures with the donor void fraction, defined by the sign of the mixture volumetric flux, makes every term of the face balance use the same fraction: the steady profile becomes smooth, and an unstable oscillation localised in the cells of the bend disappears from the linearised steady state. On the base mesh the two choices do not give the same cycle: with the mean fraction the run with the choke open needs 7.6 times as many steps and produces three blowdowns in \SI{8000}{\second} instead of four, with an interval of \SI{2440}{\second} instead of 1640 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\subsection{Conservation}\label{sec:conservation}

The staggered scheme conserves mass cell by cell in space, but conservation in time depends on the variables that the time integrator advances. Integrating the primitive form, $\partial\aG/\partial t$ and $\partial P/\partial t$, with an implicit multistep formula does not conserve the phase masses, which are non-linear functions of $\aG$ and $P$. The error is not uniform in time: it accumulates in steps, one per blowout, when a phase vanishes from many cells within a few seconds. On the application case the primitive formulation has, after \SI{4000}{\second}, created \SI{1.7}{\percent} of the liquid fed and lost \SI{1.9}{\percent} of the gas fed (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), most of it in the blowout; the pressure range of the first cycle is the same, but the inventory that sets the following cycles is no longer the one the well delivered. The conservative formulation applies the multistep formula to the masses per unit volume, $\bT(\bx) = (\rho_G\aG, \rho_L\aL)$, while Newton's method still iterates on the primitive variables,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservative}
\bT(\bx_{n+1}) - \sum_{j} a_j\, \bT(\bx_{n-j}) - \gamma h\, \bF(\bx_{n+1}) = \mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
with the Jacobian $\partial\bT/\partial\bx$ evaluated analytically. The mass balances then close to the tolerance of the Newton iterations: on the application case the errors are $5\times10^{-5}$\,\% of the liquid and $2\times10^{-5}$\,\% of the gas fed after \SI{4000}{\second}, five orders of magnitude below the primitive formulation. The map $\bT$ is singular at $\aG = -\gamma_G/\gamma_L$, so the void fraction is allowed a small undershoot, $\aG \geq -0.005$, well inside that limit. Verifying the balance itself requires care: integrating the boundary fluxes over the output times with the trapezoidal rule leaves an error of the order of a kilogram in each blowout, which is quadrature error and not loss of mass; the boundary fluxes must be integrated with the same multistep recurrence as the states.

\section{Time integration}\label{sec:time}

\subsection{Integrator}\label{sec:bdf}

The semi-discrete system is integrated by a backward differentiation formula of order one or two with variable step \citep{hairer1996}, a modified Newton method with the Jacobian reused while the convergence is fast, and an error estimate from the difference between the corrector and the predictor. Four features are not available in general-purpose stiff integrators and were needed here. The states are projected onto their physical bounds after each step, and the order falls back to one after a projection, since the history used by the second-order formula is inconsistent with a projected state. The absolute tolerance of each state is a fraction ($10^{-2}$) of the range that the state is expected to cover in the case (void fraction one, pressure up to the hydrostatic head of the riser, flow rates up to twenty times the liquid feed and fifty times the gas feed in a blowout), instead of a single value for states whose scales differ by four orders of magnitude; with a single tolerance the controller resolves the acoustic ringing of the liquid columns at a few per cent of the feed and the cost multiplies. The Newton iteration has a budget of twenty iterations, because its convergence is limited by the non-smoothness of the closures rather than by the quality of the Jacobian. The boundary fluxes are integrated with the same recurrence as the states, which gives an exact balance (Section~\ref{sec:conservation}).

Table~\ref{tab:newton} measures the Newton iteration on three windows of \SI{400}{\second} of the last cycle of the run with the choke open (Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}): the blockage, the blowout and its end. The reference is the configuration of the application: a backtracking line search on the scaled residual while the correction is above the Newton tolerance \citep{dennis1996}, and the local error estimated from the quadratic predictor with the Milne constant. Without the line search the steps grow by 40 to 75\,\%: in the blowout the controller then rejects a quarter of the attempted steps, all because the Newton iteration stalls or diverges from the predictor, which overshoots where a front crosses a cell; the exact, reused Jacobian is not the limitation. The line search must stop once the correction has converged: applied in every iteration, it refuses a converged correction whose residual does not fall --- at the kinks of the closures the residual sits on a floor of the order of the correction itself --- and the blowout window ends at the minimum step. The error estimate matters less in these windows: with the linear predictor and $r/(1+2r)\,(\bx - \bx^{pred})$, which is first order, the steps are within 7\,\% of the reference, because the step is limited by the Newton iteration and not by the error; on the model problem of Section~\ref{sec:toy}, where the error limits the step, the Milne estimate saves 20 to 50\,\% of the steps at equal accuracy and restores the second-order scaling of the cost with the tolerance. Re-evaluating the Jacobian whenever the contraction is slow halves the steps of the blowout for 2.4 times the Jacobians, and starting the iteration from the previous state instead of the extrapolated predictor does not help.

\subsection{Steady states, continuation and adaptive end time}\label{sec:steady}

Steady states are computed by Newton's method on $\bF(\bx) = \mathbf{0}$. From a generic initial estimate, the iteration does not converge for this problem; from a state reached by a transient that settles, or from the steady state of a neighbouring point, it converges in a few iterations, and continuation in the choke opening gives the steady state at every opening, stable or not. Pseudo-transient continuation is not used, because it converges only to stable equilibria \citep{kelley1998}. Transients that start from a steady state are perturbed by \SI{0.1}{\percent} in pressure, and their time step is bounded by \SI{1}{\second}. Without that bound the error control lets the step grow to hundreds of seconds while the solution is still close to the equilibrium, and a backward differentiation formula with $|\lambda h| \gg 1$ damps an unstable mode as well as a stable one: the trajectory stays on an unstable equilibrium and the run is classified as stable. The transients of the application start instead from the blocked state of Section~\ref{sec:case}, which reaches the cycle directly. The simulated time is adaptive: the integration proceeds in windows of \SI{2000}{\second} and stops when the inlet pressure stays within \SI{0.1}{\bar} or when two consecutive windows have the same amplitude and period within \SI{10}{\percent}, up to \SI{8000}{\second}. Even with the step bounded, a perturbation of \SI{0.1}{\percent} of the pressure that grows at \SI{e-3}{\per\second} needs more than \SI{2000}{\second}, the length of a window, to exceed a range of \SI{0.1}{\bar}, so that a transient from the steady state, stopped by such a rule, misses the slowest instabilities near the onset; the stability of a steady state is better decided by its eigenvalues (Section~\ref{sec:linear}).

\subsection{What breaks without each ingredient}\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} removes one ingredient at a time from the configuration of the application, with the choke wide open at the base point, and reports whether \SI{8000}{\second} are completed, at what cost, with which mass error and with which cycle (the variants are defined in Table~\ref{tab:ablationchanges}; the reference completes four blowdowns in \num{11100} steps). Three ingredients are indispensable. The slug unit with the film at equilibrium: with the slug unit of the thesis the run fails at \SI{330}{\second}, after \num{937000} steps, and with its mass-consistent version at \SI{610}{\second}, before the first cycle. The local bubble criterion: with the bubbly criteria evaluated on the gas flow rate the run completes, but needs 7.7 times as many steps and gives a different cycle (five blowdowns, 12.0 to \SI{32.6}{\bar}), because a cell with a few per cent of gas at the bend follows the gas flow rate into the intermittent closures. And the donor face closures: with the mean void fraction the run needs 7.6 times as many steps and gives three longer cycles instead of four. The other ingredients cost steps without changing the cycle: 2.3 times as many without the acoustic viscosity, 1.8 without the low-void rule, 1.6 without the virtual mass, 1.9 without the line search and 14 with the first-order formula. The primitive time integration completes the same cycle but creates \SI{0.5}{\percent} of the liquid fed and loses \SI{0.07}{\percent} of the gas, against \num{3e-5} per cent for the conservative residual. The bubble drag of the thesis changes the physics: the run completes in half the steps but with six shorter cycles (\SI{1050}{\second} instead of 1640) and a pressure range of 16.7 to \SI{30.7}{\bar}, since a drag without $g$ and $\sigma/\rho_L$ holds less gas in the riser; the slug frequency of the thesis and sharper logistic switches ($k = 80$) change the cycle little (intervals of 1770 and \SI{1580}{\second}). The line search of the Newton iteration must stop at convergence: applied in every iteration it fails at \SI{2040}{\second} (Section~\ref{sec:bdf}); and the second-order error estimate, neutral in the windows of Table~\ref{tab:newton}, costs \SI{19}{\percent} more steps than the first-order one over the whole run --- in a trajectory limited by the Newton iteration rather than by the error, the honest estimate buys accuracy, not steps. Three more variants close the table. A single absolute tolerance of $10^{-6}$ for every state completes the same cycle in 5.8 times the steps, resolving the acoustic ringing of the liquid columns that the range-scaled tolerances ignore. The low-void threshold of 0.01 instead of 0.025 gives the same cycle at the same cost (\num{11300} steps): the higher threshold is chosen for well-posedness alone (Section~\ref{sec:trajectories}). And the interfacial-pressure term of \citet{bestion1990}, added in every pattern, gives the same cycle in \SI{16}{\percent} fewer steps --- a regularisation that costs nothing here but has no physical basis in the patterns where it acts, and is left out.

\section{Compiled backend}\label{sec:backend}

The fluid properties, closures, flow-pattern identification, schemes and network equations are written once against an injected array backend: the same code runs with NumPy and with JAX \citep{bradbury2018}, where the right-hand side is compiled just in time and its Jacobian is obtained exactly by forward-mode automatic differentiation \citep{griewank2008}. The two backends agree to a relative $10^{-14}$. Three rules make the physics differentiable and compilable: no in-place assignment, loops with a data-independent structure, and branches on values written as selections, with guards before functions with restricted domains so that tangents remain finite (a product $0 \times \infty$ in a tangent gives a NaN that only appears in the Jacobian). Table~\ref{tab:performance} compares the backends. The compiled right-hand side is three to four times faster than the NumPy one, and the exact Jacobian eight times faster than the coloured finite differences \citep{curtis1974} that need 29 evaluations of the right-hand side for the 288 states of the base mesh; \SI{2000}{\second} of severe slugging run in \SI{54}{\second} instead of \SI{383}{\second} with NumPy, seven times faster, in \num{3177} steps against \num{2410} (the two Jacobians lead the Newton iteration along different paths). The compilation of the right-hand side and of the Jacobian takes 14 to \SI{31}{\second} on a cold start, \SI{2}{\second} with the compilation cache, and is paid once per case and mesh (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). The exact Jacobian does not reduce the number of Newton iterations, which is set by the non-smoothness of the closures, so the gain is in the cost per step. On a CPU the compiled code is limited by latency, not by throughput: the systems have one to a few hundred unknowns, and parallelism pays between simulations, which is how the batches of Section~\ref{sec:application} were run.

\section{Severe slugging in a well--pipeline--riser system}\label{sec:application}

\subsection{Case}\label{sec:case}

The system is a production system in \SI{300}{\metre} of water (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}, Table~\ref{tab:geometry}): a vertical well of \SI{1500}{\metre} (\SI{127}{\milli\metre} internal diameter) that receives the reservoir fluids through a linear inflow performance relation, a flowline of \SI{2000}{\metre} on the sea bed (\SI{152.4}{\milli\metre}), the last \SI{500}{\metre} of which descend at \SI{2}{\degree} to a low point \SI{17.5}{\metre} below the wellhead, a circular bend of \SI{5}{\metre} radius that turns the pipe from \SI{-2}{\degree} to the vertical, a vertical riser of \SI{317.5}{\metre} to the surface and a horizontal section of \SI{10}{\metre} to the choke, which discharges into a separator at \SI{10}{\bar}. The well, the fluid, the diameters and the water depth are those of a case of the literature on compositional steady-state simulation of production systems \citep{carvalho2022}, with two changes: the final descent was added to the flowline, since a flowline that reaches the riser base horizontally does not hold a liquid seal, and the flowline was shortened from \SI{6}{\kilo\metre} to \SI{2}{\kilo\metre}. The period of severe slugging is set by the time to pressurise the gas in the flowline and is proportional to its volume; the shorter flowline gives cycles three times shorter at the same place in the severe-slugging region of the criterion of \citet{boe1981}, in which the superficial liquid velocity must exceed $P\,U_{SG}/(\rho_L g \alpha_G L)$: about \SI{0.11}{\metre\per\second} for this flowline (\SI{0.04}{\metre\per\second} for \SI{6}{\kilo\metre}) against the 0.26--\SI{0.31}{\metre\per\second} of the production during the blocked phase. The base mesh has 15 cells in the well and 35 in the flowline--riser pipe: 8 in the horizontal section, 5 in the descent, 4 in the bend, 16 in the riser and 2 at the top.

The fluid is the light oil of that case (\SI{673}{\kilogram\per\cubic\metre} at standard conditions, \SI{78}{\degree} API) with its associated gas, described in the simulator as a polytropic pair fitted to a Peng--Robinson flash at \SI{40}{\celsius}: the densities and viscosities at \SI{20}{\bar}, the pressure dependence of the gas fitted between 10 and \SI{60}{\bar} (Table~\ref{tab:parameters}). Since the simulator has neither mass transfer nor an energy equation, the division of mass between the phases is frozen at the conditions of the flowline: the oil carries the solution gas of \SI{20}{\bar} and \SI{40}{\celsius}, $R_s = \SI{23.2}{\cubic\metre\per\cubic\metre}$ at standard conditions, and the rest of the gas is free. The well is described with the variables of the field: a productivity index $J$ of stock-tank oil, a reservoir pressure $P_{res}$ and a gas--oil ratio $\mathrm{GOR}$ at standard conditions. With the standard densities of the oil, of the dissolved gas and of the free gas ($\rho_{o}$, $\rho_{gd}$, $\rho_{gf}$) the mass per standard cubic metre of oil is $m_o = \rho_o + \rho_{gd} R_s + \rho_{gf}(\mathrm{GOR} - R_s)$, the mass fraction of free gas is $w_G = \rho_{gf}(\mathrm{GOR} - R_s)/m_o$, and the inflow relation of Section~\ref{sec:bc} has $J_L = (1 - w_G)\,J m_o/86400$ and $J_G = w_G\,J m_o/86400$ in \si{\kilogram\per\second\per\bar}. More or less gas from the well is a higher or lower GOR with the same oil and the same free gas. The base point of the analysis is a weak well, $J = \SI{5}{\cubic\metre\per\day\per\bar}$, with $P_{res} = \SI{200}{\bar}$ and $\mathrm{GOR} = \SI{50}{\cubic\metre\per\cubic\metre}$ ($w_G = 0.029$), which produces about \SI{4}{\kilogram\per\second}: the flowline then runs stratified and the riser can hold a liquid seal against the reservoir, the two conditions of severe slugging. A productive well at the same pressure (the \SI{42.9}{\cubic\metre\per\day\per\bar} that reproduce the nominal point of the reference case) delivers 10 to \SI{20}{\kilogram\per\second}, the flowline runs intermittent and the riser never blocks; a well at a lower reservoir pressure dies when the riser blocks, since the liquid columns of the riser and of the well exceed the reservoir pressure. Drier and wetter wells, up to the GOR of the reservoir fluid itself (\SI{122}{\cubic\metre\per\cubic\metre}, $w_G = 0.099$), are explored in Section~\ref{sec:gor}. The choke has $c_v = 150$, which gives a pressure drop below \SI{1}{\bar} at \SI{5}{\kilogram\per\second} when fully open.

The transients start from a blocked state constructed by the case generator (Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}): the flowline stratified at the equilibrium level of the nominal flow rates, the bend and the riser full of liquid, the well at the no-slip void fraction, the pressure marched from the separator with the local mixture density, and the total flow rate solved from the inflow relation. Filling the flowline from a no-slip start takes hours of simulated time; the blocked start reaches the first blowout within the first \SI{300}{\second}. All transients of this section start at $t = 0$ from this state and run for \SI{16000}{\second}. The first \SI{8000}{\second} are a transient: with the choke open the system goes through five cycles of decreasing period, from 2050 to \SI{1300}{\second}, before settling from about \SI{8600}{\second} on the limit cycle described next; the signatures of Tables~\ref{tab:cycle} and \ref{tab:gor} are measured over the last \SI{8000}{\second}, Fig.~\ref{fig:spacetime} shows whole runs and Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy} the last complete cycle of one of them. The only runs that start from a saved state are the windows of Table~\ref{tab:newton}, which measure the integrator on three phases of that cycle.

\subsection{The cycle with the choke open}\label{sec:anatomy}

With the choke fully open the system does not settle. Fig.~\ref{fig:anatomy} shows the last complete cycle of the run, which has period two: blowouts of two sizes alternate, and the double cycle lasts about \SI{3000}{\second} (1250 and \SI{1870}{\second} in the cycle shown). At the end of the large blowout the riser is empty (its mean liquid holdup falls to 0.22 and the top of the liquid column is at the low point), the flowline inlet pressure is at its minimum of \SI{14.7}{\bar} and the liquid seal upstream of the low point is about \SI{100}{\metre} long. The riser refills from its base over some \SI{1100}{\second}, in steps that follow the arrival of liquid slugs from the flowline, while the seal grows to \SI{360}{\metre} and the inlet pressure rises almost linearly, by \SI{12}{\bar} per \SI{1000}{\second}: the gas produced by the well (\SI{0.12}{\kilogram\per\second}) accumulates in a flowline that runs stratified over most of its length and intermittent near the seal, and the riser is a bubbly column that reaches the top some \SI{300}{\second} before the blowout. When the inlet pressure reaches \SI{31}{\bar} --- the separator pressure plus the \SI{20.1}{\bar} of a liquid-filled column of \SI{317}{\metre} plus the friction of the seal --- the seal starts to move. The first blowout is partial: the liquid is pushed over the top at up to \SI{33}{\kilogram\per\second}, ten times the mean production; the gas reaches the riser base and expels the upper two thirds of the column, whose top falls to about \SI{100}{\metre} above the low point and whose mean holdup falls to 0.28; and the inlet pressure falls only to \SI{22}{\bar}, since the lower third of the column stays in place. The riser refills in some \SI{450}{\second}, the pressure rises again to \SI{30}{\bar} over \SI{900}{\second}, and the second blowout is complete: \SI{45}{\kilogram\per\second} of liquid and \SI{1.1}{\kilogram\per\second} of gas through the choke, the riser left annular at the top and then empty, the pressure back to \SI{14.7}{\bar} --- which resets the cycle. The two blowouts take about a tenth of the double period. The mean production over the last \SI{8000}{\second} is \SI{3.86}{\kilogram\per\second}, against the \SI{3.60}{\kilogram\per\second} that the well delivers in the steady state which the choke stabilises at $a = 0.1$ (Section~\ref{sec:opening}): the cycle does not cost production on average, it concentrates it in surges of ten times the mean. Fig.~\ref{fig:cycleprofiles} follows the cycle with profiles along the whole system at six instants, and Table~\ref{tab:cycle} gives its signature.

\subsection{Closing the choke}\label{sec:opening}

Fig.~\ref{fig:spacetime} shows the liquid holdup along the pipe against time for three openings, from the blocked start, with the flowline inlet pressure and the liquid outflow below, and Table~\ref{tab:cycle} the signature of the cycles for the five openings of the batch over the last \SI{8000}{\second}. Closing the choke to 0.6 keeps severe slugging but removes the period doubling: the cycle becomes regular, with a period of \SI{1240}{\second}, the pressure between 21.7 and \SI{30.6}{\bar}, a seal of \SI{215}{\metre} and a surge of \SI{32}{\kilogram\per\second} (8.6 times the mean), and the riser no longer empties (its mean holdup stays above 0.54), because the choke, which takes up to \SI{4}{\bar} during the surge, holds back the column. At 0.3 the cycle changes character: the riser stays full of liquid (holdup above 0.64, the column always at the top), the seal is \SI{120}{\metre} long, the period falls to \SI{730}{\second} and the inlet pressure oscillates between 25.7 and \SI{28.5}{\bar}; what leaves the riser is a pulse of \SI{10}{\kilogram\per\second}, 2.7 times the mean, in every cycle --- a pressure cycle with liquid pulses rather than a blowout. At 0.2 the pressure cycle survives with the same period (\SI{720}{\second}) and an amplitude of \SI{0.7}{\bar}, with the outflow smoothed to 1.2 times the mean; at 0.1 the system is steady at \SI{33.0}{\bar}, with the choke taking \SI{5.8}{\bar}. The opening that stabilises the riser is therefore between 0.1 and 0.2, where the choke takes 2 to \SI{6}{\bar} against the \SI{20}{\bar} of the riser column (Section~\ref{sec:linear} locates it), and the mean production changes little along the way: between 3.7 and \SI{3.9}{\kilogram\per\second} for openings from 1 to 0.2, and \SI{3.60}{\kilogram\per\second} at 0.1, where the choke adds its own back-pressure to the well.

\subsection{More and less gas from the well}\label{sec:gor}

The gas--oil ratio of the well sets the gas that accumulates in the flowline during the blocked phase. Fig.~\ref{fig:gor} and Table~\ref{tab:gor} compare the base point (GOR 50, $w_G = 0.029$) with a drier well (GOR 30, barely above the solution gas, $w_G = 0.007$) and with wetter ones (GOR 80, $w_G = 0.059$, and the \SI{122}{\cubic\metre\per\cubic\metre} of the reservoir fluid itself, $w_G = 0.099$), all with the choke open. The three wetter wells give the same severe slugging: the riser empties at every large blowout (mean holdup down to 0.17), the pressure swings between 15 and \SI{31}{\bar} and the surge reaches 40 to \SI{45}{\kilogram\per\second}, since both are set by the riser column and not by the gas; the gas rate sets the mean period, \SI{1530}{\second} with GOR 50 and 80 (both with period doubling) and \SI{1180}{\second} with GOR 122, and the mean production, which grows from 3.9 to \SI{4.3}{\kilogram\per\second} through the lighter well column. The dry well is different in kind: its gas never clears the riser, whose column stays liquid-continuous (holdup above 0.70) while the flowline pressure drifts between 26.5 and \SI{30.6}{\bar} and the liquid leaves in pulses of \SI{11}{\kilogram\per\second}, three times the mean, every \SI{1760}{\second} --- a pressure cycle with liquid pulses, as at $a = 0.3$ with the base well, with the gas bubbling through a riser that never blows out. With the gas of the reservoir fluid the flowline still runs stratified during the blocked phase: at this flowline length and these rates the system stays well inside the severe-slugging region of the criterion of \citet{boe1981} (Section~\ref{sec:case}), and the end of severe slugging at high gas rates is not reached.

\subsection{Linear stability}\label{sec:linear}

Newton's method on $\bF = \mathbf{0}$ converges on the steady state of the run at $a = 0.1$ in a few iterations, and continuation in the opening gives the steady state at every opening up to the fully open choke (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}, Table~\ref{tab:linear}). Two global pairs of the generalised spectrum govern its stability. A pair with a period of about \SI{70}{\second} --- the oscillation of the liquid column in the riser against the gas pocket of the flowline --- crosses the imaginary axis at $a_H = 0.13$; a slow pair with a period of 500 to \SI{1000}{\second} --- the exchange of liquid between the flowline and the riser, which becomes the cycle --- crosses at $a \approx 0.20$, and its period grows with the opening, from \SI{545}{\second} at 0.2 to \SI{965}{\second} at 0.38, close to the \SI{720}{\second} of the simulated pressure cycles at 0.2 and 0.3. Above $a \approx 0.4$ the slow pair collides on the real axis and the steady state has a real unstable mode, the static runaway of the riser, whose growth rate reaches \SI{0.046}{\per\second} with the choke open: the steady state is left within minutes and the blocked start is the natural way to the cycle. The transients agree with the spectrum: at 0.1 the run settles on the steady state, at 0.2 it carries the \SI{720}{\second} pressure cycle of \SI{0.7}{\bar} amplitude, and from 0.3 the cycles of Table~\ref{tab:cycle}; the \SI{70}{\second} mode, although linearly unstable from 0.13, is not visible in the runs at 0.2 and 0.3. At $a_H = 0.13$ the pressure drop across the choke in the steady state is \SI{3.7}{\bar}, against the \SI{20.1}{\bar} of the liquid-filled riser; the criterion of \citet{jansen1996}, evaluated with the flowline gas fraction (0.37) and the riser holdup (0.70) of that steady state, asks for \SI{3.5}{\bar} --- the same number --- and for \SI{5.4}{\bar} with a riser kept full of liquid. Beyond the threshold the steady states exist and are unstable; the cycles straddle their flowline inlet pressure (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}c) and match their production on average (Fig.~\ref{fig:linear}d).

\section{Practical recommendations}\label{sec:lessons}

The difficulties met along the way are common to transient two-fluid codes; the following recommendations summarise what was measured.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A numerical method that does not require hyperbolicity does not make an ill-posed model usable: ill-posedness appears as growth that increases with mesh refinement. Check the characteristics on the states the simulation visits, and regularise with physical terms where it goes.
\item Collocated schemes without dissipation on the void waves, and backward differences applied to all variables, cannot carry the fronts of a severe-slugging cycle; a staggered scheme with phase-wise donor cells can.
\item Use the same void fraction in every term of a face momentum balance; mixing the donor value with the mean creates an odd--even mode.
\item Do not limit the void fraction to keep it in bounds; limit the fluxes of the phase that the donor cell does not have, and relax the vanishing phase towards no slip, not towards rest.
\item Integrate conservative variables. Verify the mass balance with the boundary fluxes integrated by the same recurrence as the states, never by quadrature of the outputs.
\item Flow-pattern criteria built for steady flow must be evaluated on the local state, with smooth switches of relative width and weights that form a partition of unity, before they can be used in a transient implicit solver.
\item Closure terms that are dimensionally inconsistent may compensate each other at one scale and fail at another; check them at more than one pressure and diameter.
\item A choke law with a mass-weighted mixture density does not resist gas and is singular in reverse flow; use the no-slip volume-weighted density.
\item A closed-box integrator prevents conservative residuals, projection on physical bounds and range-scaled tolerances, all of which were needed; an exact Jacobian does not reduce Newton iterations when the closures are not smooth, but compiling the right-hand side reduces the cost of each one.
\item When a transient starts on, or settles near, an equilibrium, bound the time step: an implicit multistep formula with steps much longer than the time scale of an instability damps it, and the run reports a stable state that is not. Even then, a slow instability may not grow enough within a simulated window; decide the stability of an equilibrium from its eigenvalues.
\item Linearise a conservative formulation with its mass matrix, $\bJ\bv = \lambda\bM\bv$. The eigenvalues of $\bJ$ alone show fast unstable modes that do not exist and hide the slow pair whose crossing of the imaginary axis marks the onset of the cycles; with the mass matrix the steady state predicts the onset and the period of the cycles near it, and transient simulations are needed for the amplitude of the cycles and where they coexist with a stable steady state.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

A four-equation two-fluid model with the flow pattern identified dynamically along the whole system simulates severe slugging in a well--pipeline--riser system once six conditions are met. The closures must be regularised where the simulation goes: with a low-void rule, an objective virtual-mass term and an interfacial pressure in the slug unit the model is hyperbolic in all but \SI{0.1}{\percent} of the states that a severe-slugging cycle visits. The slug unit must take its film from the physics of the film, and the bubbly criteria of the pattern identification must read the void fraction of the cell: with the slug unit of the thesis the field case does not reach its first blowout, and with the bubbly criteria on the gas flow rate it costs eight times as many steps and changes its cycle. The discretisation must be staggered, with phase-wise donor cells, donor face closures and a donor-cell limiter of the mass fluxes: mean face closures create an odd--even steady state, an unstable oscillation of the cells at the bend and a different cycle at eight times the cost. The time integration must conserve the phase masses: the primitive form creates \SI{0.5}{\percent} of the liquid and loses \SI{0.07}{\percent} of the gas over five cycles of the field case. The integrator must project the states on their bounds, scale its tolerances with the ranges of the states, bound its step near an equilibrium and search along the Newton direction only until the correction has converged. And the flow-pattern criteria must be evaluated on the local state with smooth switches. A compiled right-hand side with exact Jacobians makes a transient seven times cheaper than with NumPy, and four hours of slugging cost four to eight minutes.

The linearisation of the conservative formulation requires its mass matrix. With it, the steady state of the discretised system predicts the onset of the cycles. Two global pairs cross the imaginary axis as the choke opens: the oscillation of the riser column against the gas pocket of the flowline, with a period of a minute, at an opening of 0.13, and the exchange of liquid between flowline and riser, with a period of ten minutes that is the period of the simulated pressure cycles, at 0.19; with the choke open a real mode, the static runaway of the riser, dominates and the cycle is a relaxation cycle with complete blowout of the riser. At the onset the pressure drop across the choke is \SI{3.7}{\bar} against a riser head of \SI{20}{\bar}, the value that the criterion of \citet{jansen1996} predicts from the gas fraction of the flowline and the holdup of the riser. On the field case the cycle with the choke open has period two --- blowouts of 33 and \SI{45}{\kilogram\per\second}, ten times the mean production, alternate every 1250 and \SI{1870}{\second} while the flowline pressure swings between 15 and \SI{31}{\bar} --- and is reached from the blocked start after five transient cycles; closing the choke to 0.6 makes the cycle regular, to 0.3 turns it into a pressure cycle with liquid pulses that no longer empties the riser, and to 0.1 stabilises the system at \SI{33}{\bar} with a loss of \SI{7}{\percent} of the production. The gas--oil ratio of the well sets the period of the cycle, inversely to the free-gas rate, but neither its pressure range nor its surge, which the riser column sets; the dry well, barely above its solution gas, never clears the riser and cycles in pressure with liquid pulses.

\section{Closure relations}\label{app:closures}

Wall and interfacial shear stresses are $\tau = f\rho v|v|/2$, with $|v|$ regularised as $(v^2 + \epsilon_v^2)^{1/2}$, $\epsilon_v = \SI{e-4}{\metre\per\second}$, so that they are smooth at rest, and the Fanning factor of \citet{churchill1977} on a hydraulic diameter $D_h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:churchill}
f = 2\left[\left(\frac{8}{Re}\right)^{12} + \left(c_1 + c_2\right)^{-3/2}\right]^{1/12}, \quad c_1 = \left[2.457\ln\frac{1}{(7/Re)^{0.9} + 0.27\,\varepsilon/D_h}\right]^{16}, \quad c_2 = \left(\frac{37530}{Re}\right)^{16},
\end{equation}
with $Re = \rho D_h|v|/\mu$ and $\varepsilon$ the wall roughness. The forces per unit length in Eq.~\eqref{eq:momentum} are $\Gamma_L = \tau_{wL}S_L - \tau_i S_i$ and $\Gamma_G = \tau_{wG}S_G + \tau_i S_i$, with the interfacial stress evaluated on the slip $v_r = v_G - v_L$. The acceleration of gravity in the closures is $g = \SI{9.81}{\metre\per\square\second}$.

\paragraph{Stratified flow} The wetted angle $\beta$ follows from $\beta - \sin\beta = 2\pi\aL$ and the level from $h_L = D[1 - \cos(\beta/2)]/2$. The perimeters are $S_L = \beta D/2$, $S_G = \pi D - S_L$ and $S_i = D\sin(\beta/2)$, the hydraulic diameters $D_L = 4A_L/S_L$ and $D_G = 4A_G/(S_G + S_i)$, and the wall factors are Eq.~\eqref{eq:churchill} at $(v_k, D_k)$. The interfacial stress is $\tau_i = f_i\rho_G v_r|v_r|/2$ with $f_i = f_G\,[1 + 15\,(h_L/D)^{1/2}\max(0, |v_{GS}|/v_{GT} - 1)]$ and $v_{GT} = \SI{5.5}{\metre\per\second}$ \citep{andritsos1987}. The pressure corrections are the hydrostatic heads between the interface and the centroids of the two phase areas, measured from the pipe bottom,
\begin{gather}
\Delta P_L = \aG\,\Delta P_{LG}, \quad \Delta P_G = -\aL\,\Delta P_{LG}, \quad \Delta P_{LG} = \left[(\rho_L - \rho_G)h_L - \rho_L z_L + \rho_G z_G\right] g\cos\theta, \\
z_L = D\left[\frac12 - \frac{\sin^3(\beta/2)}{3\pi\aL}\right], \qquad z_G = D\left[\frac12 + \frac{\sin^3(\beta/2)}{3\pi\aG}\right],
\end{gather}
whose derivative with respect to $\aG$ is the level-gradient term that keeps stratified flow hyperbolic.

\paragraph{Annular flow} A liquid film of thickness $\delta = D(1 - \aG^{1/2})/2$ wets the whole wall: $S_L = \pi D$, $S_G = 0$ and $S_i = \pi D\aG^{1/2}$, with $D_L = 4A_L/S_L$ and $D_G = 4A_G/S_i$. The film factor follows \citet{kowalski1987}, $f_L = \max[24/Re_L,\ 0.263\,\aL^{-1}(D_L/(Re_L D))^{1/2}]$ with $Re_L = \rho_L D_L|v_L|/\mu_L$, and the interfacial factor is the stratified one with $h_L = \delta$. Both phases are at the mixture pressure ($\Delta P_k = 0$).

\paragraph{Bubbly flow} The bubble diameter is $d_b = \min(d_e, d_{max})$, with $d_e = (16\sigma/g\Delta\rho)^{1/2}$ and the breakup diameter $d_{max} = [0.725 + 4.15|v_{GS}/v_M|^{1/2}](\sigma/\rho_L)^{3/5}(2 f_M |v_M|^3/D)^{-2/5}$ \citep{hinze1955,calderbank1958}, where $v_M = v_{LS} + v_{GS}$ and $f_M$ is Eq.~\eqref{eq:churchill} with the liquid properties at $v_M$ on $D$. The terminal velocity $v_b = (2.14\sigma/\rho_L d_b + 0.505\,g d_b)^{1/2}$ \citep{mendelson1967,fan1990} sets the drag coefficient $C_D = 4 g d_b \Delta\rho/(3\rho_L v_b^2)$. With $S_i = \pi D^2\aG/d_b$ and $\tau_i = \tfrac38 C_D\,\rho_L v_r|v_r|/2$ the interfacial force per unit length is $\tfrac34 C_D \aG\rho_L A\,v_r|v_r|/d_b$, which balances buoyancy at the terminal velocity. Only the liquid has wall friction, over $S_L = \pi D\aL$ with Eq.~\eqref{eq:churchill} at $(v_L, D)$. The liquid pressure correction is $\Delta P_L = 0.25\rho_L v_r^2$ \citep{stuhmiller1977}, with $|v_r|$ limited to \SI{3}{\metre\per\second}, and $\Delta P_G = 0$.

\paragraph{Intermittent flow} A slug unit consists of an aerated liquid slug, a fraction $r$ of the unit, with gas fraction $\alpha_s$, and a film region, the fraction $1 - r$, with gas fraction $\alpha_f$ \citep{taitel1990b}; the slug is treated with the bubbly closure and the film region with the stratified one, and each closure term is the average $r X_s + (1 - r) X_f$. The correlations are written for co-current flow and use $u_{LS} = |v_{LS}|$, $u_{GS} = |v_{GS}|$ and $u_M = u_{LS} + u_{GS}$; the local velocities take back the sign of the superficial velocity of their phase. The slug gas fraction follows \citet{barnea1985}, limited to the gas content of the cell,
\begin{equation}
\alpha_s = \min(\alpha_s^0, \aG), \qquad \alpha_s^0 = 0.058\left[2\left(\frac{0.4\,\sigma}{g\Delta\rho}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{2 f_S u_M^3}{D}\right)^{2/5}\left(\frac{\rho_L}{\sigma}\right)^{3/5} - 0.725\right]^2, \qquad 0 \le \alpha_s^0 \le 0.52,
\end{equation}
with $f_S$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:churchill} for the liquid at $u_M$ on $D$, and the translational velocity is $v_t = 1.2\,u_M + (0.35\sin\theta + 0.54\cos\theta)(gD)^{1/2}$ \citep{bendiksen1984}. The gas fraction of the film region comes from the physics of the film rather than from a slug-length correlation: in horizontal and downward sections it is the stratified equilibrium $\alpha_f^{st}$ of \citet{taitel1976} at the superficial velocities of the cell (the same equilibrium used by the flow-pattern criteria of Appendix~\ref{app:patterns}); in upward sections the film falls around a Taylor bubble, and $\alpha_f^{TB}$ solves the kinematics of the unit with the free-falling film of Brotz \citep{taitel1980},
\begin{equation}
\left[v_t + c\,(1 - \alpha^{1/2})^{1/2}\right](1 - \alpha) = v_t(1 - \aG) - u_{LS}, \qquad c = 9.916\,(g D\max(\sin\theta, 0))^{1/2};
\end{equation}
the two blend over the first \ang{10} of upward inclination, $\alpha_f^{eq} = (1 - w)\alpha_f^{st} + w\,\alpha_f^{TB}$ with $w = u^2(3 - 2u)$, $u = \min(1, \max(0, \theta/\ang{10}))$. The film never holds more than $\alpha_{f,max} = 0.95$ of gas (beyond that the cell is annular, which the pattern weights cover), and never less than the cell,
\begin{equation}
\alpha_f = \max\left[\min(\alpha_f^{eq}, \alpha_{f,max}),\ \aG\right], \qquad r = \frac{\alpha_f - \aG}{\alpha_f - \alpha_s} \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
so that the unit conserves the gas of the cell, $\aG = r\alpha_s + (1 - r)\alpha_f$, reduces to a liquid slug at the state of the cell when $\aG \le \alpha_s^0$ and to a film region at the state of the cell when $\aG \ge \alpha_f^{eq}$. The local velocities follow from the mass balances of the unit: in the slug $u_L = [u_{LS} + v_t(\aG - \alpha_s)]/(1 - \alpha_s)$ and $u_b = [u_{GS} - v_t(\aG - \alpha_s)]/\alpha_s$, in the film region $u_f = v_t - (v_t - u_L)(1 - \alpha_s)/(1 - \alpha_f)$ and $u_G = v_t - (v_t - u_b)\,\alpha_s/\alpha_f$; they return the superficial velocities of the cell, $u_{LS} = r(1 - \alpha_s)u_L + (1 - r)(1 - \alpha_f)u_f$ and likewise for the gas. The slug front picks up the slower film and accelerates it, a loss per unit of $\Delta p_a = \rho_L(1 - \alpha_s)(\alpha_f - \alpha_s)(v_t - u_L)^2/(1 - \alpha_f)$ \citep{taitel1990b}; with the frequency of units passing a point, $\omega_s/v_t$ per metre, it adds $A\,\Delta p_a\,\omega_s/v_t$ to the force on the liquid, against its direction, with the slug frequency of \citet{gregory1969} and the inclination factor of \citet{zabaras2000},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zabaras}
\omega_s = 0.0226\left[\frac{u_{LS}}{gD}\left(\frac{19.75}{u_M} + u_M\right)\right]^{1.2}\left[0.836 + 2.75\,\max(\sin\theta, 0)^{1/4}\right] \quad\text{(SI units)},
\end{equation}
applied with the horizontal factor in downward pipes. The loss vanishes smoothly when the unit degenerates ($r$ within $0.05$ of $0$ or $1$), since without a film or without a slug there is no front.

\section{Flow-pattern criteria}\label{app:patterns}
\setcounter{equation}{0}\setcounter{table}{0}\setcounter{figure}{0}

The criteria are evaluated at every cell with the local state: the level $\tilde h = h_L/D$ and the angle $\beta$ of the cell's void fraction, and $\aG$, $\aL$ of the cell. The Froude numbers are $F_L = [\rho_L/(\Delta\rho\,g D)]^{1/2}|v_{LS}|$ and $F_G = [\rho_G/(\Delta\rho\,g D)]^{1/2}|v_{GS}|$, with $g = \SI{9.8}{\metre\per\square\second}$ (\SI{9.81}{\metre\per\square\second} elsewhere), and $f_S$ and $f_L$ are Eq.~\eqref{eq:churchill} for the liquid at $v_{LS}$ and at $v_{LS}/\aL$ on $D$. Each comparison $X > X_c$ is the logistic function
\begin{equation}
\sigma(X, X_c) = \left[1 + \exp\left(-k\,\frac{X - X_c}{|X_c|}\right)\right]^{-1}, \qquad k = 20,
\end{equation}
and the direction of the mixture is $s = \tfrac12[1 + \tanh(v_M/v_s)]$ with $v_s = \SI{0.02}{\metre\per\second}$. A pipe is horizontal within \ang{10} of the horizontal and vertical within \ang{10} of the vertical. The criteria are
\begin{align}
&\text{Kelvin--Helmholtz, } s = 1: && \frac{F_G}{\cos^{1/2}\theta} > \left[\frac{\pi(1 - \tilde h)^2\aG^3}{4\sin(\beta/2)}\right]^{1/2}, \label{eq:kh}\\
&\text{film trajectory, } s = 0: && \left(\frac{f_S F_L^2}{\cos\theta}\right)^{1/2} > \frac{(1 - \tilde h)^{1/2}\aL}{(f_L/f_S)^{1/2}},\\
&\text{wind waves, } s = 1: && F_G\left(\frac{Re_{SL}}{\cos\theta}\right)^{1/2} > 20\,\aL^{1/2}\aG, \qquad\text{gravity waves, } s = 0:\ F_L > 1.5\,\tilde h^{1/2}\aL,\\
&\text{film bridging:} && \aL^{film} < 0.35, \qquad \aL^{film} = \max(\aL^{eq}, \aL),\\
&\text{annular Froude, vertical:} && F_G > \left\{\frac{75}{Eo_D^2}\left[\left(1 + \frac{(Eo_D/75)^2}{0.0065}\right)^{1/2} - 1\right]\right\}^{1/2}, \quad Eo_D = D\left(\frac{\Delta\rho\,g}{\sigma}\right)^{1/2},\\
&\text{bubble packing:} && \alpha_b < 0.25 \text{ (bubbly)}, \quad \alpha_b > 0.52 \text{ (intermittent)},\\
&\text{dispersed bubbles:} && d_{max} < d_{def} = 2\left(\frac{0.4\,\sigma}{\Delta\rho\,g}\right)^{1/2}, \quad d_{max} < d_{migr} = \frac38\frac{\rho_L}{\Delta\rho}\frac{f_M v_M^2}{g\cos\theta},\\
&\text{turbulent dispersion, horizontal:} && \left(\frac{2 f_S F_L^2}{\cos\theta}\right)^{1/2} > \left[\frac{2\pi\aL^2\aG}{\sin(\beta/2)\,(f_L/f_S)}\right]^{1/2},
\end{align}
where $Re_{SL} = \rho_L D|v_{LS}|/\mu_L$, $\aL^{eq}$ is the film fraction of the annular equilibrium of the superficial velocities \citep{taitel1980,barnea1987}, $\alpha_b = \aG$ is the void fraction of the cell (an estimate from the gas flow rate, $|v_{GS}|/(|1.25\,v_M + u_b| + 10^{-3})$ with $u_b = 1.53(\sigma g\Delta\rho/\rho_L^2)^{1/4}\sin\theta$, misclassifies cells with little gas and a large gas flow rate at the riser bend and is not used), $\max_\varepsilon(a, b) = [a + b + ((a - b)^2 + \varepsilon^2)^{1/2}]/2$ with $\varepsilon = 0.02$ is the smooth maximum used for the film fraction, and $d_{max}$ is the breakup diameter of the bubbly closure with $f_M$ evaluated at the density of the drift-flux mixture. With $\sigma_{KH}$, $\sigma_{tr}$, \dots\ the logistic values of the criteria, the weights of Eq.~\eqref{eq:weights} are
\begin{gather}
w_S = \left\{(1 - \sigma_{KH})\,s + \left[1 - \sigma_{tr}(1 - s)\right](1 - s)\right\}(1 - \chi_V), \qquad p_A = 1 - (1 - p_A^0)(1 - \sigma(\aG, 0.9)), \\
p_A^0 = (1 - \sigma_{br})\left[(1 - \chi_V) + \sigma_{Fr}\,\chi_V\right], \qquad p_B = \left[\sigma_{<0.25} + \sigma_{disp}(1 - \sigma_{>0.52})^2(1 - \sigma_{<0.25})^2\right](1 - \chi_H) + \sigma_T\,\chi_H,
\end{gather}
with $\chi_H$ and $\chi_V$ the horizontal and vertical indicators, $\sigma_{br} = \sigma(\aL^{film}, 0.35)$ and $\sigma_{disp}$ the product of the two dispersed-bubble criteria; the wavy part of stratified flow has the weight $w_S[\sigma_K s + \sigma_{F_L}(1 - s)]$. Below $\aG = 0.02$ the weights blend into the bubbly ones with $w = u^2(3 - 2u)$, $u = \min[1, \max(0, (2\alpha_* - \aG)/\alpha_*)]$ and $\alpha_* = 0.01$ (low-void rule).
