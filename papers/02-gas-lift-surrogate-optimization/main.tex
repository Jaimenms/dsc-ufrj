\begin{abstract}
A gas-lifted satellite well with a flowline and a riser can produce in severe slugging, in a pressure cycle or in a steady state depending on two operating decisions, the opening of the topside choke and the pressure of the lift gas at the platform, and the time-averaged production of an unstable operating point is not what a steady-state model predicts for it. This paper builds the data and the surrogate models of those two decisions and uses them to optimise the operation of the well. The well, flowline, riser and gas-lift branch are simulated with the four-equation two-fluid model of a companion study, with the flow pattern identified dynamically and the gas-lift line, annulus and check valve simulated with the same model and controlled only by the platform gas pressure. Three sampling campaigns over the two decisions, in rounds of Sobol' points every one of which starts from the final state of the base run, with an adaptive horizon that detects the established regime and captures three cycles, produced 230 settled runs in two days on a laptop over 20 to \SI{260}{\bar} of lift gas, plus 23 budget-limited runs in the corner of high opening and high gas pressure that are re-run with a longer budget; each run is reduced to time-averaged markers (mean, minimum, maximum and the two extreme frequencies) of pressure, void fraction and phase flow rates at eight characteristic points of the network. The map of the decisions shows a stability boundary that runs from \SI{80}{\bar} of lift gas with the choke nearly closed to \SI{125}{\bar} with it open, continuous injection modulated by the cycle below it, and a production gain of \SI{34}{\percent} above it at \SI{160}{\bar} and \SI{40}{\percent} at \SI{250}{\bar}. Two surrogates of the mean liquid, gas and lift-gas rates and of their spans were trained on the markers: a neural network and a physics-informed network whose physics are the time-averaged mass balances of the well, the gas-lift branch and the valve, exact for the averages and evaluated at collocation points without simulation. With 161 training runs and an early stop set by a scan of the training length, both networks predict the mean liquid rate with a test coefficient of determination of 0.89, the gas and lift-gas rates with 0.999, the maximum lift-gas rate with 0.998 and the maximum liquid rate with 0.84 to 0.90; the physics reduce the residuals of the well and annulus balances three to four times and divide the error of the gas rates by three with ten to forty training runs, while the spans of the rates, the signature of slugging, remain the quantity that neither network predicts reliably. The surrogate then enters an operational optimisation of the two decisions: the revenue of the mean liquid rate minus the cost of compressing the lift gas from \SI{5}{\bar} to the platform pressure, with the predicted maxima of the lift-gas and liquid rates bounded by the compressor and separator capacities. At the prices of the example the compression costs one per cent of the revenue and the optimum is the open choke with about \SI{240}{\bar} of lift gas, interior in the gas pressure and on a plateau that is flat to one per cent from 205 to \SI{255}{\bar} and across the last fifth of the choke opening, \SI{6}{\percent} above the best point of the first campaign; a compressor capacity moves the optimum down the valve law to the edge of the stable region, and the trade-off against the suction pressure \tbd{is left for the next step}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Continuous gas lift is the artificial-lift method of most deepwater satellite wells in Brazil, and its optimisation --- how much gas to inject in each well of a field that shares a compressor --- has a large literature built on steady-state performance curves \citep{souza2010}. A well with a flowline and a riser, however, does not always have a steady state to be optimised: with the choke open and little gas it produces in severe slugging \citep{schmidt1980,taitel1986,jansen1996}, and the time-averaged production, the liquid surges that reach the separator and the pressure swings at the wellhead are quantities of the cycle, not of the unstable steady state that a nodal model would return for the same point. A companion study \citep{souza2026a} developed a four-equation two-fluid model with dynamic flow-pattern identification that reproduces the cycle in a well--flowline--riser system of field dimensions, located the onset of slugging as the choke opens and showed, with a gas-lift branch added to the system, that injection at 100 to \SI{120}{\bar} regularises the cycle and from \SI{130}{\bar} suppresses it.

This paper is about the operation of that gas-lifted well. Its question is practical: given the two decisions an operator has, the opening of the topside choke and the pressure of the lift gas at the platform, what does the well deliver on average, with what surges and in which regime --- and can a model cheap enough for optimisation answer that over the whole plane of the two decisions? The dynamic simulation is too expensive to be called inside an optimiser (minutes per point), so the paper builds a surrogate, and it does so with a method that follows the companion work on compositional steady-state surrogates \citep{carvalho2026b}: a multilayer perceptron trained on simulated data, and the same network with a physics term \citep{raissi2019,karniadakis2021}. The novelty is on both sides of the surrogate. On the data side, the targets are not profiles but time-averaged signatures of a dynamic simulation, and producing them in affordable time required a campaign design of its own: an adaptive horizon that recognises the established regime, warm starts along a shortest path through the sampled points, and a storage that keeps only what the training needs. On the physics side, the governing equations cannot be imposed on a time average of a cycling system; what can, exactly, are the time-averaged mass balances of the well, of the gas-lift branch and of the valve, and the paper shows that this much physics is what the data alone cannot supply.

Section~\ref{sec:model} summarises the phenomenological model and the gas-lift branch. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the campaign, the horizon, the markers and the resulting map of the decisions. Section~\ref{sec:surrogates} presents the two surrogates and Section~\ref{sec:results} compares them. Section~\ref{sec:optimisation} formulates the operational optimisation on the surrogate, with capacity constraints on the predicted maxima, and reports its first results.

\section{The phenomenological model}\label{sec:model}

The system is the well--flowline--riser of the companion study \citep{souza2026a} with its gas-lift branch (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}): a vertical well of \SI{1500}{\metre} (\SI{127}{\milli\metre}) fed by a linear inflow performance relation, a flowline of \SI{2000}{\metre} (\SI{152.4}{\milli\metre}) whose last \SI{500}{\metre} descend at \SI{2}{\degree} to a low point, a bend of \SI{5}{\metre} radius, a riser of \SI{317.5}{\metre} and \SI{10}{\metre} of horizontal pipe to a choke that discharges into a separator at \SI{10}{\bar}. The gas-lift branch is a 4-in line that runs from the platform along the route of the production line to the wellhead, the casing--tubing annulus (9~5/8~in by 5~1/2~in) to a gas-lift valve \SI{30}{\metre} above the bottom of the well, and a check valve fed by a chamber at the base of the annulus. The base well has a reservoir pressure of \SI{200}{\bar}, a productivity index of \SI{5}{\cubic\metre\per\day\per\bar} and a gas--oil ratio of \SI{50}{\cubic\metre\per\cubic\metre}; the fluid is the light oil of \citet{carvalho2022} frozen as a polytropic pair.

Every pipe, the gas pipes included, is described by the one-dimensional four-equation two-fluid model of the companion study: mass and momentum of each phase, closures by flow pattern (stratified, intermittent, bubbly, annular) selected dynamically on the local state through smooth logistic switches, a low-void rule, an objective virtual-mass term and an interfacial pressure in the slug unit that keep the model hyperbolic along the simulated trajectories. The staggered finite-volume discretisation with phase-wise donor cells and the conservative variable-step implicit integration of that study are kept unchanged, with a compiled right-hand side and exact Jacobians; the base mesh has 15 cells in the well, 35 in the flowline--riser pipe, 19 in the gas-lift line and 10 in the annulus, 323 states in all. The gas-lift branch is controlled by one number, the gas pressure $P_{gas}$ at the platform end of the line: the injected rate is not prescribed but follows from $P_{gas}$, the static gas columns of the line and the annulus, the valve law $q = 0.0075\,C_V\sqrt{\rho_G\,\Delta P}$ ($C_V = 1.5$, $\Delta P$ in bar, regularised at the origin) and the pressure of the well at the injection point, so that it responds to whatever the well does; at \SI{20}{\bar} the valve never opens and the system is the base case of the companion study with the branch inert. The reservoir gas is the gas of the well; the lift gas adds to it at the injection point.

\section{Data: campaign, horizon and markers}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sampling the two decisions}\label{sec:sampling}

The inputs are the choke opening $a \in [0.1, 1]$ and the platform gas pressure $P_{gas}$, with the well fixed at its base state, in three campaigns: a first over 20 to \SI{160}{\bar}, a second over 160 to \SI{260}{\bar} once the first had placed the optimum of Section~\ref{sec:optimisation} on its upper edge, and a third over 160 to \SI{260}{\bar} restricted to openings of 0.5 to 1, the region of that optimum. Points are drawn from a scrambled Sobol' sequence \citep{sobol1967,owen1998} in rounds of 25, so that the rounds of a campaign together remain a single space-filling design, and each point is identified by a hash of its input tuple; only what the training needs is stored, the markers below and the final state of the run. The initial condition of every run is the same: the final state of the base run of the companion study, \SI{8000}{\second} of severe slugging with the choke open and the gas line at \SI{20}{\bar}. The campaigns began with a different rule, one chain per round in which the first point started from the base state and every later point from the final state of the previous one, along the shortest open path through the 25 points on the inputs rescaled to $[-1, 1]$; the rule spares the transient of a cold start, but above an opening of 0.5 with more than \SI{100}{\bar} of lift gas every run started from a neighbour exhausted its budget while every run started from the base state settled in about a minute, and the campaigns were completed from the base state. A steady-state initial condition, the natural alternative, is not attainable for this model: its steady equations are discontinuous in the state, because the closures switch flow pattern at the base of the riser, and a Newton iteration converges only from a state that already has the right pattern in each cell, which a settled dynamic run provides and no algebraic estimate does. The transient from the base state costs 160 to 2000 steps where the well is steady and up to 16000 where it cycles, and it does not depend on the order of the points, so the runs of a round are distributed over processes without any sequencing.

\subsection{Adaptive horizon with capture of cycles}\label{sec:horizon}

Each run integrates in windows of \SI{3000}{\second} and monitors two signals, the pressure at the wellhead and the liquid rate through the choke. After a minimum time (\SI{4000}{\second} from a blocked start, \SI{1500}{\second} from a warm start) the regime is taken as established when both signals are permanent (amplitude below \SI{0.1}{\bar} and \SI{0.05}{\kilogram\per\second}) or when two consecutive windows have the same envelope, that is, the same amplitude, mean, minimum and maximum within \SI{10}{\percent} of the amplitude; the envelope criterion, rather than equality of periods, is what accepts the cycles whose blowouts alternate in size. When a monitored signal cycles with a period that fits ten times in the window, the comparison window shrinks to ten periods (at least \SI{600}{\second}) and is evaluated on the series already integrated, so that a fast cycle is accepted about \SI{1200}{\second} after it appears instead of two full windows later. The run then continues for three more cycles of the longest monitored period (\SI{600}{\second} for a steady state), which are the capture window of the markers, and stops; a run that never settles stops at \SI{24000}{\second} and is kept with the markers of its last \SI{4000}{\second} and a flag. A wall-clock budget of \SI{40}{\minute} per run, raised to \SI{60}{\minute} for the re-runs of the points that had exhausted it, bounds the points where the integrator crawls: with the choke more than half open and more than \SI{100}{\bar} of lift gas the liquid outflow at the top of the riser oscillates with a period under a minute and an amplitude of up to \SI{15}{\kilogram\per\second}, and the steps fall to 0.3 to \SI{0.6}{\second} with a quarter of them rejected; the short horizontal section between the riser and the choke, the open point of the closures that the companion study reported, is not the cause, since the same runs without it cost the same. Those runs are flagged and excluded from the training.

\subsection{Markers at characteristic points}\label{sec:markers}

A run is reduced to markers of its capture window at eight points of the network: the bottom of the well, the injection point, the wellhead, the low point of the flowline, the top of the riser, the two ends of the gas-lift line and the base of the annulus. At each point four signals are recorded --- pressure, void fraction, liquid and gas mass flow rates --- and each signal is summarised by its mean, minimum, maximum and two frequencies, the inverse of the longest and of the shortest interval between successive maxima of the smoothed signal: equal for a periodic cycle, different when blowouts alternate, zero for a permanent signal. The gas injected through the valve is recorded in the same way, and a class is assigned from the wellhead pressure and the choke liquid rate as in the companion study (steady, pressure cycle or severe slugging when the peak liquid rate exceeds twice its mean), with a fourth class, the fast cycle, when the pressure cycles with a period under \SI{300}{\second}: it is the hydrodynamic intermittency of the riser top of Section~\ref{sec:horizon}, which the peak criterion alone would read as severe slugging although the wellhead pressure swings by a fraction of a bar; the settled severe-slugging and pressure cycles of the campaigns all have periods above \SI{540}{\second}. Trajectories are not kept: they would cost \SI{12}{\mega\byte} per run against \SI{10}{\kilo\byte} of markers, and the surrogate consumes only the markers.

\subsection{The map of the decisions}\label{sec:map}

Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarises the three campaigns and Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset_map} shows them on the plane of the decisions. Of the 230 runs that settled, 97 produce in severe slugging, 13 in a pressure cycle and 120 in a steady state; 116 settled on a repeated cycle, 112 on a steady state and 2 reached the time limit; 23 further runs exhausted the budget of \SI{40}{\minute} and are kept apart (below). The stability boundary is a diagonal that runs from about \SI{90}{\bar} of lift gas with the choke at 0.3 to 100 to \SI{125}{\bar} with it open: the more open the choke, the more gas is needed to suppress the cycle, and at the smallest openings some throttled points are steady without lift gas. The check valve opens between 86 and \SI{94}{\bar} almost independently of the choke, so that below \SI{90}{\bar} the data are the base well with the choke as the only decision; between the opening of the valve and the boundary the injection is continuous and modulated by the cycle, which shortens and regularises; above the boundary the well is steady and its production rises with the gas, to \SI{5.3}{\kilogram\per\second} at \SI{160}{\bar} with the choke open, \SI{34}{\percent} above the base case, and on to \SI{5.6}{\kilogram\per\second} at \SI{250}{\bar} with the choke half open, a tenth of the gain per bar of the first hundred bar above the boundary. The peak liquid rate through the choke reaches \SI{45}{\kilogram\per\second} in the slugging region with the choke open and stays below \SI{6}{\kilogram\per\second} in the steady region. The 23 runs that exhausted the budget sit in a strip of openings from 0.5 to 0.99 between 106 and \SI{244}{\bar}, where the riser receives the most gas and the liquid outflow cycles fast (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}). Seven of them, re-run from the base state, settled in a steady state within two minutes --- their first failure was the warm start of the early campaigns --- and the others sit on the fast cycle and need the longer budget; none enters the training until it settles, and they mark where the simulator, not the well, is least efficient.

\section{Surrogates}\label{sec:surrogates}

\subsection{Outputs and network}\label{sec:network}

The surrogate maps the two decisions to eleven quantities of the capture window: the mean liquid and gas rates at the top of the riser, the mean gas-lift rate through the valve, the spans (maximum minus minimum) of the same three rates, which are the signature of slugging, the maxima of the liquid rate at the top and of the gas-lift rate, which the capacity constraints of the optimisation use, and three auxiliary mean pressures --- at the bottom of the well, at the injection point and at the base of the annulus --- that the physics of the next section needs. Spans and maxima are learnt as $\log(1 + x)$, so that they are never negative and their orders of magnitude are resolved. The network is a multilayer perceptron \citep{hornik1989} with two hidden layers of 32 hyperbolic-tangent units and a linear output, inputs and outputs standardised with the training statistics, trained with Adam \citep{kingma2015} in full batch for 2000 epochs on the mean squared error of the standardised outputs, with the epochs unrolled in a compiled scan so that a network trains in a few seconds; 70\,\% of the 230 settled runs train it and 30\,\% test it. The number of epochs is the early stop of a scan from 1000 to 16000: the test error of the mean liquid rate is flat between 1000 and 2000 epochs and rises steadily beyond, for both networks, as the fit of the training runs approaches $R^2 = 1$ --- with 150 runs and 1200 weights the training time is the regularisation, and the physics-informed network degrades more slowly than the data-only one. A grid of one or two layers, 8 to 64 units and three learning rates (24 networks of each kind, Section~\ref{sec:results}) checked that the architecture is not critical.

\subsection{Physics of the averages}\label{sec:physics}

A physics-informed network adds to the data loss the residual of the governing equations at collocation points that need not be simulated \citep{raissi2019}. Here the targets are time averages of a dynamic system, and the time average of a cycle does not satisfy the two-fluid equations, which are nonlinear; what it satisfies exactly are the balances that are linear in the averaged quantities. Over a periodic cycle or a steady state, the mass of each phase in the system does not change on average, so that
\begin{align}
R_1 &= \bar q_L - J_L\,(P_{res} - \bar P_{bh}), \label{eq:r1}\\
R_2 &= \bar q_G - J_G\,(P_{res} - \bar P_{bh}) - \bar q_{gl}, \label{eq:r2}\\
R_3 &= \bar P_{an} - \kappa\,P_{gas}, \label{eq:r3}\\
R_4 &= \bar q_{gl} - 0.0075\,C_V\sqrt{\rho_G(\bar P)\,\langle \bar P_{an} - \bar P_{inj}\rangle_+}, \label{eq:r4}
\end{align}
where $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the liquid and gas balances between the linear inflow relation at the mean bottom-hole pressure and the mean rates at the top, $R_3$ is the static gas column from the platform to the base of the annulus ($\kappa = 1.138$ for this fluid and depth), and $R_4$ is the valve law written with the mean pressures, the only approximate relation of the four (the mean of a square root is not the square root of the mean, but the modulation of the valve pressure drop is within \SI{25}{\percent} and the error is a few per cent), with a smooth gate that closes the valve when the mean annulus pressure falls below the mean injection pressure. The loss is the data term plus $\lambda$ times the mean square of the four residuals, each scaled by the standard deviation of the quantity it compares, evaluated on 200 Latin-hypercube points \citep{mckay1979} of the plane of the decisions; $\lambda = 1$, and values from 0.1 to 100 give the same fit with residuals decreasing with $\lambda$. The residuals are closed-form functions of the network outputs, so that the gradient of the physics term is exact and cheap, unlike the detached-Jacobian construction that the compositional surrogates needed \citep{carvalho2026b}. The information in the physics is therefore not the dynamics but the accounting: the network is told that whatever enters the well on average leaves it on average, and that the lift gas is set by two mean pressures it also predicts.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:surrogate} and Fig.~\ref{fig:parity} compare the two networks on the 69 test runs. Both networks predict the mean gas rate at the top and the mean gas-lift rate with $R^2$ of 0.999 and the three mean pressures with 0.99 to 1.00; the mean liquid rate, 0.89 for both (root-mean-square error of \SI{0.24}{\kilogram\per\second}), is the hardest of the means: it varies little over the plane (2.2 to \SI{5.6}{\kilogram\per\second}), its time average over three cycles of a surging signal is the noisiest of the targets, and the third campaign, dense in the steady corner, adds points where it changes by a few per cent across the plane. With the first campaign alone (105 training runs) the data-only network reached only 0.57 on it and the physics raised it to 0.88; with the three campaigns and the early stop the two networks are equal on every mean, and the physics show in the balances: the scaled residuals of $R_1$ (liquid inflow) and $R_3$ (annulus column) fall by factors of four and three, while $R_2$ and $R_4$, which both networks satisfy to within 0.05 standard units, do not separate them. The maxima behave like the means they ride on: the maximum lift-gas rate reaches 0.998 with both networks, since the injection is at most modulated by a quarter, and the maximum liquid rate, which carries the surge, 0.90 and 0.84. The spans are the weak point of both: the liquid span scores between 0.6 and 0.9 depending on which runs fall in the test set, with either network ahead on one split and behind on the next, and the gas span and the span of the injected gas stay below 0.3, since the amplitude of a surge in three cycles is a noisy marker by nature. Fig.~\ref{fig:maps} shows the physics-informed surrogate on the plane of the decisions: the mean liquid rate rises with the lift gas above the boundary and falls as the choke closes, the lift-gas rate is zero below \SI{90}{\bar} and rises with $P_{gas}$ as the valve law dictates, and the maximum liquid rate marks the slugging region, with surges of 20 to \SI{40}{\kilogram\per\second} below the boundary against 5 above it, and a narrow band of artefacts along the boundary, where the data change class within a few bar.

\section{Operational optimisation}\label{sec:optimisation}

\subsection{Formulation}\label{sec:formulation}

The operator chooses the choke opening $a$ and the platform gas pressure $P_{gas}$ to maximise the daily margin of the well,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fob}
F(a, P_{gas}) = c_{oil}\,Q_o(\bar q_L) - c_{e}\,24\,W(\bar q_{gl}, P_{gas}),
\end{equation}
the revenue of the oil minus the cost of compressing the lift gas, subject to the capacities of the compressor and of the separator,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constraints}
\max q_{gl} \le Q_{gl}^{cap}, \qquad \max q_L \le Q_{L}^{cap},
\end{equation}
written on the maxima of the rates over the cycle, which the surrogate predicts (Section~\ref{sec:network}), and not on their means: a compressor and a separator are sized for peaks. The oil rate $Q_o$ follows from the mean liquid rate with the liquid mass per stock-tank cubic metre of the base fluid (\SI{700}{\kilogram\per\cubic\metre}, oil and solution gas); the compression power $W$ is that of a polytropic compression of the mean lift-gas rate from the suction pressure (\SI{5}{\bar}) to $P_{gas}$ in stages of ratio at most four with intercooling to the suction temperature ($k = 1.3$, \SI{313}{\kelvin}, efficiency 0.75, molar mass \SI{19.5}{\gram\per\mole} from the gas density of the fluid). All the quantities in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:fob} and \eqref{eq:constraints} come from the physics-informed surrogate, so that $F$ and the constraints are smooth closed-form functions of the two decisions, evaluated in microseconds. The problem is solved by enumeration on a $201 \times 201$ grid of the plane restricted to the feasible nodes, followed by a sequential quadratic programming refinement with the two constraints and the bounds, and the optimum is confirmed by one run of the simulator at that point.

\subsection{First results}\label{sec:optresults}

\tbd{[Seed of the optimisation study; the Pareto front against the suction pressure and the real capacities are pending.]} Fig.~\ref{fig:fob} shows the objective on the plane of the decisions for an oil price of \SI{70}{\$} per barrel and an energy price of \SI{0.10}{\$} per \si{\kilo\watt\hour}, with the runs of the campaigns by class. On the first campaign alone the optimum was the corner of maximum gas and open choke, $(1, \SI{160}{\bar})$, with a margin of \num{290e3}~\$/d, and the simulator run at that point, started from the base state, confirmed the surrogate to \SI{2}{\percent} (a mean liquid rate of 5.40 against \SI{5.38}{\kilogram\per\second}, a lift-gas rate of 1.17 against 1.15 and a bottom-hole pressure of 67.0 against \SI{67.2}{\bar}); an optimum on the edge of the sampled box says only that the box is too small, which is why the second and third campaigns were run. With the three campaigns the optimum is interior in the gas pressure: $(a, P_{gas}) = (1.0, \SI{242}{\bar})$, with a margin of \num{308e3}~\$/d, a revenue of \num{312e3}~\$/d against a compression cost of \num{3.6e3}~\$/d (\SI{1490}{\kilo\watt} for \SI{1.85}{\kilogram\per\second} of lift gas), \SI{6}{\percent} above the corner of the first campaign. The compression is one per cent of the revenue, so the objective is the production, which the surrogate puts at a maximum of \SI{5.74}{\kilogram\per\second} where the gain of the lift gas is balanced by the friction of a tubing and riser that carry 1.9 kg of gas per second; the optimum is a plateau rather than a peak, flat to one per cent from 205 to \SI{255}{\bar} with the choke open and to half a per cent between openings of 0.8 and 1, and it falls by \SI{5}{\percent} at \SI{160}{\bar}; successive fits of the surrogate move the optimum along this plateau by \SI{20}{\bar}, which is the resolution the data give it. The open choke is the physical bound, not the edge of the sampled box: a closed choke holds back the gas, and the production at the plateau is insensitive to the last tenth of the opening. The simulator run at $(1.0, \SI{224}{\bar})$, on the plateau \SI{0.3}{\percent} below the optimum and started from the base state, reproduces the surrogate in the means: over its last \SI{4000}{\second} the liquid rate is 5.79 against the predicted \SI{5.72}{\kilogram\per\second}, the gas rate 1.88 against 1.88, the lift-gas rate 1.71 against 1.71, the bottom-hole pressure 58.4 against \SI{58.8}{\bar} and the annulus pressure 254 against 255. It does not reproduce the maximum: the run sits on the fast cycle of the riser top described in Section~\ref{sec:horizon}, with the liquid rate through the choke swinging between 2 and \SI{16}{\kilogram\per\second} every minute while the wellhead pressure stays within \SI{0.3}{\bar}, and the surrogate, trained on the runs that settled, predicts a peak of 5.7. The optimum therefore sits in the strip of budget-limited runs of Section~\ref{sec:map} for a physical reason, the fast cycle, not for a numerical one, and the objective, which depends on the means, is confirmed to \SI{1}{\percent}; a separator constraint written on the peak would need the fast cycle in the training data, which asks for a budget of about \SI{90}{\minute} per run in that strip. A second run on the plateau, at $(0.94, \SI{205}{\bar})$, agrees with the surrogate in the means and pressures to better than \SI{1}{\percent}, and there the fast cycle has an amplitude of only \SI{0.6}{\kilogram\per\second}, so its peak (5.9 against a predicted 5.8) is also reproduced: the amplitude of the fast cycle varies across the strip. At \SI{260}{\bar} with the choke open the same well is steady, and stays steady when its run is restarted from the settled state. The optimum is insensitive to the oil price, since the compression stays one per cent of the revenue (at \SI{20}{\$} per barrel it moves only to \SI{228}{\bar}), and leaves the plateau only through the energy price: at \SI{2}{\$} per \si{\kilo\watt\hour} the compression is a seventh of the revenue and the optimum retreats to \SI{173}{\bar}, and at \SI{5}{\$} per \si{\kilo\watt\hour} it jumps below the opening of the valve, $(0.77, \SI{85}{\bar})$, the best point of the well without lift gas, since the margin of the lift gas no longer pays its compression.

The constraints change the picture as soon as they bind, and the compressor binds first. The valve law makes the lift-gas rate a function of $P_{gas}$ alone, so a compressor capacity fixes the platform pressure and the optimum walks down the valve law as the capacity shrinks, with a separator that accepts peaks of \SI{20}{\kilogram\per\second} never active: with \SI{1.5}{\kilogram\per\second} the optimum is $(1.0, \SI{200}{\bar})$ with a margin of \num{304e3}~\$/d, one per cent below the unconstrained one, since the plateau is flat; with \SI{1.0}{\kilogram\per\second} it is $(1.0, \SI{142}{\bar})$ and \num{284e3}~\$/d, \SI{8}{\percent} below; with \SI{0.8}{\kilogram\per\second} it is \SI{123}{\bar} and \num{260e3}~\$/d, \SI{16}{\percent} below and just above the stability boundary, with a predicted peak liquid rate of \SI{4.6}{\kilogram\per\second}. Along the constraint the objective is nearly indifferent to the opening above 0.7 (it loses one per cent at 0.9 and three to six per cent at 0.5, and successive fits of the surrogate place the optimum anywhere between 0.7 and 1), so the choke stays open. A smaller compressor pushes the optimum across the boundary: with \SI{0.5}{\kilogram\per\second} it goes to about \SI{102}{\bar}, in the slugging region, with surges of \SI{14}{\kilogram\per\second} that a separator of 20 still accepts; with a separator of \SI{6}{\kilogram\per\second} the separator constraint takes over and holds the optimum at \SI{104}{\bar} with the choke open, at the edge of the cycle, which is what a constraint written on the peak, not on the mean, is for.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

A dynamic two-fluid simulation of a gas-lifted well can feed a surrogate of its operating decisions once the campaign is designed for it: one fixed initial condition for every point, the final state of the base run, which the integrator of this model tolerates where a warm start from a neighbour or a Newton steady state does not, an adaptive horizon that recognises the established regime and captures three cycles, and markers at characteristic points instead of trajectories. Over the plane of the choke opening and the platform gas pressure the well has a stability boundary from 90 to \SI{125}{\bar} of lift gas, continuous injection modulated by the cycle below it and \SI{34}{\percent} more production above it at \SI{160}{\bar}, \SI{40}{\percent} at \SI{250}{\bar}. Of the two surrogates trained on the 230 runs, the physics-informed one, whose physics are the time-averaged mass balances of the well and of the gas-lift branch, predicts the mean liquid rate with $R^2$ 0.89, like the data-only network, the gas and lift-gas rates with 0.999 and their maxima with 0.84 and 0.997, satisfies the well and annulus balances three to four times better than the data-only network, needs a third of the data for the same error on the gas rates, and leaves the spans of the rates as the quantity to be handled by the class and the stability map rather than by regression. On this surrogate the optimisation of the revenue minus the compression cost, with the predicted maxima bounded by the compressor and separator capacities, is a two-variable problem solved by enumeration and refined in a fraction of a second: at the prices of the example the compression is one per cent of the revenue and the optimum is the open choke at about \SI{240}{\bar}, on a plateau flat to one per cent from 205 to \SI{255}{\bar}, where the gain of the lift gas is balanced by the friction it costs; a compressor capacity makes the valve law the active constraint and moves the optimum down the platform pressure to the stability boundary, and a separator capacity written on the peak liquid rate is what keeps it out of the cycle. \tbd{The trade-off against the suction pressure, as a Pareto front, is the next step.}
